## The Surprise: There Is No Secret, and No Bill

Somewhere above the Pacific right now, a satellite the size of a small car is sweeping a 3,000-kilometre-wide stripe of the Earth below it. It will cross the same spot again within a day. The photograph it takes is not classified, not paywalled and not hidden — it is pushed to a public image server within hours, tile by tile, at an address any browser can request. This is the least-known open-data miracle on the internet: **live satellite views of the whole planet are free**, and have been for over a decade.

The catch — the one that makes people distrustful when they finally try it — is the word *live*. Nobody streams raw satellite video to your map. What you get is something better for everyday use and worse for Hollywood: a **daily composite**, a single stitched image of the whole Earth assembled from every pass a sensor made in one calendar day, refreshed like clockwork. Understanding that one distinction explains everything about what you are looking at.

## What the Layers Actually Are

Three different kinds of "satellite view" get conflated constantly, so here is the honest inventory:

- **True-color daily imagery.** Instruments like VIIRS aboard the Suomi NPP and NOAA-20 satellites measure sunlight reflecting off the surface and clouds across hundreds of spectral bands, and public systems like NASA's GIBS (Global Imagery Browse Services) publish it as map tiles within hours of acquisition. Clouds, dust plumes, smoke from wildfires, green forests and brown deserts all appear as your eye would see them from orbit. If you look at a spot and think "that cloud was not there an hour ago" — correct, and that is by design.
- **Night lights.** A separate infrared band listens for what glows after dark: city grids, fishing fleets, gas flares, occasionally a wildfire's true extent. The classic "Earth at night" posters come from monthly versions of exactly this data; the daily version shows which cities slept and which stadiums hosted a final.
- **High-resolution street-level zoom.** Companies like Esri and Maxar tile commercial imagery at roughly 0.3–10 metres per pixel. You can read a car roof but not a licence plate, and you are looking at a photo taken whenever that patch was last flown over — sometimes months ago. It is a library of the Earth, not a camera on it.

Then there is the **Blue Marble** — a hand-composited static portrait of the planet, cloud-patched from many days, used as a pretty base layer precisely because it never changes. It is a painting of Earth, not a photo, and knowing that makes it more impressive, not less.

## Why "Daily" Is Better Than "Real-Time"

A genuine live feed would show one narrow stripe at a time as the satellite happens to pass over — most of the planet would be blank TV static. A daily composite instead guarantees *complete coverage*: every square kilometre photographed in the last 24 hours, geometrically corrected and stitched. For the things people actually check — where is the smog, did the cyclone eye close over, which side of the island is green — completeness beats freshness. The sensors also correct for the awkward angle of the horizon, so the map you see is the world measured straight down.

For the truly impatient, real-ish alternatives exist: geostationary weather satellites (Himawari, GOES) sit over one spot and image the same hemisphere every ten minutes, which is why typhoon-tracking sites can show a storm *moving*. What they cannot show is the ground beneath thick cloud — the polar-orbiting daily passes can.

## Trying It Properly

Open [Tukuk-OS Maps](/maps) and switch the base layer to the live view, or skip straight to [Live Earth](/maps/sky) — a full-window version with the daily true-color pass, the night-lights band, Blue Marble and street-level zoom stacked in one panel. A few exercises that make the data click:

1. **Find weather, not landmarks.** At country zoom on the true-color layer, clouds are the story. Watch the Indian Ocean or the Atlantic during storm season and you are seeing the same imagery meteorologists brief governments from.
2. **Flip to night lights and look at your border.** The line where one country's grid brightness drops to another's is one of the most quietly political images available to the public — the Korea DMZ, India–Pakistan at Diwali, the Nile seen as a single lit filament.
3. **Search any place and fly there** on Live Earth, then toggle layers over the same spot: geography in true color, human footprint at night, maximum detail in the high-res layer. The same square of land tells three unrelated stories depending on which sensor looked at it.
