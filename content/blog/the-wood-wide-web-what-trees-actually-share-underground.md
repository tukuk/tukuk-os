TITLE: The Wood Wide Web: What Trees Actually Share Underground
EXCERPT: Forest trees are joined by a fungal internet that trades sugar for minerals, warns neighbours of drought, and lets stumps keep feeding for years. The science is real, the popular version is exaggerated — here is what mycorrhizal networks genuinely do.

## The Partnership Inside Almost Every Root Tip

Start with the fact that makes everything else possible: roughly ninety per cent of land plants grow with fungi threaded into their root cells. The exchange is blunt economics — the tree pays the fungus in sugars from photosynthesis, and the fungus pays in water and mineral ions, especially phosphorus, gathered by hyphae far thinner than any root and mining a soil volume a plant could never reach alone. This is the **mycorrhiza**, and without it most forests would be half as productive on most soils. A mature tree may be wired to dozens of fungal species at once, and a single cubic centimetre of forest soil can hold many kilometres of hyphae. The "individual tree" is, below ground, a negotiated composite organism.

There are two architectural schools. **Ectomycorrhizal** fungi (birch, oak, pine, dipterocarp — the great temperate and tropical forest families) sheathe the root tip without entering cells and push a lattice of hyphae between the cortex layers, building the extensive, soil-mining networks the tropics' dominant trees rely on. **Arbuscular** fungi penetrate the cells themselves, folding the membrane into treelike arbuscules where the actual sugar-for-mineral handoff happens; they serve most crops, herbs, and the majority of plant species overall. The headline-grabbing "network" claims concern the first kind.

## What the Experiments Actually Showed

The "wood wide web" hypothesis came of age in Suzanne Simard's 1997 *Nature* experiment: birch and douglas-fir seedlings in a plot, some connected underground by shared fungi, were fed radioactive carbon dioxide through their leaves, and the isotope turned up in the neighbouring species — moving both ways, more between connected plants than between isolated ones. That established the network's existence and its capacity for transport. Subsequent isotope, gene-expression, and removal studies have extended the ledger: a shaded seedling can gain a measurable carbon top-up from a lit parent tree through common mycelium; drought-stressed trees alter the allocation; and when a tree is girdled or cut, connected neighbours can keep feeding the surviving stump's root system for years — the strange vigour of stumps that stay green is partly a subsidy.

The warnings are equally well documented. Not all partners play fair: some orchids are pure mycorrhizal parasites, taking carbon for a lifetime without paying, and certain *Lactarius* and related fungi route more resources toward their own reproduction than toward any host. A network is a market, and markets contain speculators. The popular telling — mother trees nursing their young, forests as superorganisms with intentions — is extrapolation beyond the evidence: the transfer volumes are real but usually small compared to a tree's own budget, and a seedling's survival depends overwhelmingly on light, water, and its own roots.

## What Foresters and Gardeners Should Actually Do With This

Two practical conclusions survive the hype intact. First, **inoculate with local**: mycorrhizal commerce is partnership-specific, and transplanting seedlings into fungicide-sterilised, structureless soil severs an economy the seedling needs; nursery inoculum and undisturbed topsoil beat most fertiliser programmes. Second, **keep the reservoir**: mature trees in a stand are the network's hubs — the "hub tree" idea is better supported for carbon storage and fungal diversity than for maternal intent — which is why continuous-canopy retention and leaving dead wood in situ preserve soil fungal capital that clearfelling bankrupts for a generation. The dipterocarp forests of Southeast Asia regenerate this way naturally: seedlings establish in the shade of giants, wired before they are tall enough to notice.

For anyone who walks forests rather than manages them, the network changes the aesthetics into something truer. The quiet stand you are in is not a collection of individuals spaced by competition; it is a trading floor threaded with living cable, in which the line between one tree and another is a matter of convenience rather than biology. [How scientists classify life](https://tukuk.org/blog/how-scientists-classify-life-from-linnaeus-to-dna-trees) was written before any of this was known, and the fungi that make forests possible were filed, patiently and wrongly, with the plants — a reminder that taxonomy, like any network, is a record of who was trading with whom when the map was drawn.
