## An Animal, Not a Plant, Not a Rock

A coral is a colony of **polyps** — tiny cnidarians, cousins of sea anemones and jellyfish — each a soft tube of a mouth ringed with stinging cells, repeated thousands of times into a shared structure. The reef itself is the accumulated limestone skeleton those polyps secrete: a cup of calcium carbonate under each polyp's body, laid down day after day, generation after generation, so that the living tissue is a millimetre-thin skin over a monument its own ancestors built over centuries or millennia. Reef builders get their limestone from seawater: they pull dissolved calcium and bicarbonate across their tissues and precipitate aragonite crystals, an energetically expensive act that, at its fastest, grows a branching Acropora by ten to twenty centimetres a year and a massive Porites by about a centimetre. That slow geology in biological time is what makes a reef an irreplaceable object: centuries of accretion cannot be regrown on a human timescale, and a dead reef framework erodes into rubble within decades once the cementing algae that hold it together stop being supplied by living tissue.

## The Farming Partnership Inside the Tissue

The reason corals can build at all in the nutrient-poor water of the tropical shallows is a partnership. Inside each polyp's cells live millions of microscopic algae from the family Symbiodiniaceae — the "zooxanthellae" of older textbooks — which photosynthesise and hand over a large share of their fixed carbon to the host, in some conditions up to about 90 per cent of the coral's energy needs. The coral, in turn, gives the algae shelter, access to light in the transparent tissue, and a steady supply of the nitrogen and phosphorus its own waste products provide. This deal sets the reef's boundaries almost everywhere: it needs light, so reef builders live within the sunlit tens of metres; it works best in clear, warm, stable water, which concentrates the great reef belts into the shallow tropical bands around 30 degrees north and south; and it is sensitive to heat, which is the whole story of bleaching. Because the partnership drives calcification, the most productive reefs on Earth exist in ocean deserts — the reef is an engine for recycling nutrients that the surrounding water mostly lacks.

## What Bleaching Actually Is

When water gets too warm for too long — typically about one to two degrees above the local summer maximum, sustained for weeks — the algal partnership breaks down. Heat damages the photosynthetic machinery in the symbionts, which start producing reactive oxygen molecules that poison the host tissue; the coral either expels the algae or the algae lose their pigment, and the transparent animal tissue reveals white skeleton beneath. That is **bleaching**: the coral is not dead but starving, because it has lost its main energy supplier, and survival depends on how quickly the water cools and symbionts are reacquired from the water column. Bleached corals can also take on more heat-tolerant symbiont types, a limited acclimatisation route researchers watch closely. The 2023–2024 event was the largest and most geographically widespread global bleaching ever recorded, driven by record ocean heat on top of El Niño warming, with surveys reporting severe bleaching on most of the world's reef provinces — the Great Barrier Reef's northern sections, Florida, the Caribbean, the Indian Ocean, and even remote Pacific atolls. Unlike a storm, which breaks a reef in a night and lets it rebound in fifteen to twenty years if nothing else goes wrong, thermal bleaching kills across entire ocean basins at once, and repeated events in the same decade leave adult colonies too depleted to reproduce.

## Why Anyone Far From the Sea Should Care

Reefs supply direct economic and physical services to hundreds of millions of people — the frequently cited estimate is that roughly 500 million people depend on reef fisheries and reef-protected coastlines for food and shelter, a number with honest uncertainty bands but a real basis. Reef structures absorb something like ninety per cent of wave energy, so a healthy reef is cheaper coastal defence than any seawall, and their loss is measured in erosion as well as biodiversity. The drugs-and-biotechnology argument is genuine but should be kept modest: reef organisms have yielded real medicines, and the chemistry of an ecosystem we have barely sampled is an unbanked asset. What is not modest is the extinction arithmetic: reef ecologists now treat the 1.5-degree era as one in which most shallow-water reefs, as functional calcifying ecosystems, will not survive in their current form without large-scale reduction in emissions plus local relief from pollution, overfishing, and sediment. The [classification of life](https://tukuk.org/blog/how-scientists-classify-life-from-linnaeus-to-dna-trees) puts corals among the animals we most misdescribe as scenery, and [how the sea's chemistry works](https://tukuk.org/blog/why-is-the-sea-salty-and-is-it-getting-saltier) is the other half of understanding why they build. A reef is the closest thing the ocean has to architecture: a city of animals, farming its own food, that can starve while the water around it looks exactly the same as it always did.
