## The Physics That Keeps Broadcasting Free

Radio's survival starts with its economics, and its economics start with physics. An AM carrier wave bounces along the ground and, at night, off the ionosphere — reaching continents from a single transmitter; FM rides line-of-sight VHF with better fidelity but shorter range. The decisive property is **one-to-many**: a transmitter's signal costs the same whether ten or ten million people receive it, and the receiver pays nothing. Streaming inverted that — every listener bills the server individually — which is why pure-online radio struggles with the costs broadcast handles for free, and why broadcast still anchors the medium even in 2026. The tuning-fork basics — frequency, modulation, bandwidth — are the same carrier-wave ideas behind every wireless technology you use, from phone mast to satellite downlink.

## Why the "Dying" Medium Refused to Die

Radio has outlived the phonograph, the cassette, the MP3 player and three separate "podcast will kill it" waves. Its moat is not fidelity — it is **liveness and locality**: a human speaking now, in your language, about your traffic, your weather, your team, with a call-in line nobody else has. Car listening, the medium's strongest fortress, needs exactly that: eyes busy, one hand free, zero scrolling. Podcasting stole the on-demand slot but returned the commute to radio. Then radio did the expected trick and ate the threat: stations began simulcasting streams, apps like TuneIn and community directories turned every AM transmitter into a worldwide one, and internet-only "radio" adopted the live-DJ format from the other direction. A listener in Manila can now tune a village station in Norway — something broadcast physics never allowed, and the reason curated station directories matter more than ever.

## How an Internet Stream Actually Works

A modern station usually broadcasts *and* streams the same feed. Digitally, that means a live encoder turning the microphone output into a continuous compressed stream (MP3, AAC or Opus), pushed to a relay server that serves listeners in chunks; players buffer a few seconds against network hiccups — the same trade-off as video buffering, but tuned for latency so DJs and request lines stay in real time. Cheap bandwidth made global simulcast possible; it also made thousands of volunteer and community stations viable, since a stream server costs a fraction of a transmitter. The listening side is now browser-native: no plugins, just an audio tag pointed at a stream URL — which is how a station list can be genuinely one click away from live.

## Finding a Station in the Noise

The survival story ends in a discovery problem: when every station on Earth is reachable, choosing is the new tuning. Directories solve it the way indexes solved search — metadata first: country, language, genre, bit rate, and whether the stream is still alive. The radio hub at [tukuk.org/radio](https://tukuk.org/radio) works exactly that way: browsable by country and genre with live play buttons, so the world's broadcast layer is one tap from any browser. And because a stream is just bytes, listening can be honest and free — no algorithm deciding your next song; our guide to [escaping the algorithm](https://tukuk.org/blog/escape-the-algorithm-discover-music-and-radio-yourself) explains why that human-curated gap is worth defending. Radio did not survive the internet by becoming the internet; it survived by staying the thing the internet never replicated: someone, somewhere, talking live — now with the whole world able to tune in.
