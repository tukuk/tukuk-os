TITLE: Why You Get Jet Lag: The Body Clock Explained
EXCERPT: Jet lag has nothing to do with how many hours you flew and everything to do with how many time zones you crossed. Your body runs on an internal clock set to a different meridian than the one on the wall — and jet lag is the friction of re-syncing it.
TAGS: jet lag, circadian rhythm, sleep, health, science

## You Carry a Clock That Isn't Quite 24 Hours

Deep in the brain, a small structure called the **suprachiasmatic nucleus** coordinates a body-wide timing system — your **circadian rhythm**. Left alone in a bunker with no clocks or daylight, it does not keep perfect 24-hour time; it drifts to roughly **24.2 hours**. That is why, with no cues, your sleep and wake times would slide later each day. What pins it back to exactly 24 is **light**, especially the blue-rich daylight of morning, which the retina reports straight to the clock and nudges it earlier. Over your life this internal clock has been trained to your home time zone, scheduling a precise daily sequence: core body temperature, hormone release (melatonin rising at night, cortisol in the early morning), digestion, alertness and reaction time, all staggered across the hours.

## What Jet Lag Actually Is

When you cross time zones quickly, the wall clock and the world's light/dark jump to the new zone overnight — but your internal clock is still running on home time. **Jet lag is the mismatch between your circadian rhythm and the local schedule**, and every symptom follows from that desync: you feel wide awake at 3 a.m. local time (which is morning back home) and wiped out at 2 p.m. (which is the middle of your biological night). It's the same reason a long-distance flight west-to-east or east-to-west feels worse than a short one: the number of hours *flown* barely matters; the number of *zones crossed* does, because that is how far the clock must catch up. Time zones themselves are the human convention this biological clock collides with — explained in [our guide to time zones](https://tukuk.org/blog/time-zones-explained).

## Why East Is Harder Than West

Traveling **east** forces your body to fall asleep and wake up *earlier* than its rhythm wants — you must shorten a cycle that naturally runs a little long, which is harder. Traveling **west** lets you stay up later and sleep in, lengthening the cycle, which the ~24.2-hour clock does more easily. That asymmetry is why eastbound trips over many zones (say, Americas to Europe or Asia) feel worse than the same-length westbound return. The general rule of thumb is that the body resynchronises at about **one time zone per day**, so a 6-zone jump takes roughly a week to fully settle.

## How to Reset Faster

Because light is the master cue, timing it is the most powerful lever: seek bright daylight in the **local morning** and avoid it in the local evening when you need to advance your clock, and do the reverse when you need to delay it. Getting outside in the first bright hours after arrival helps more than sleeping in a dark room all day. Other cues reinforce the shift — eating at **local mealtimes**, exercising earlier in the day, and keeping a consistent wake time. Melatonin taken at the target bedtime can help some travellers advance their rhythm; it is worth understanding as a timing signal rather than a simple sedative. Small habits like limiting long daytime naps and staying hydrated keep you functional while the clock grinds into step.

## The Cost of Fighting Your Clock

Jet lag is a reminder that "24 hours" is a biological reality, not just a number on a clock face. The same internal timing governs why shift work and late-night screens feel disorienting even without any flight at all: whenever your behaviour is pushed out of sync with your circadian rhythm, the body notices. The good news is that the clock is always trying to re-sync — jet lag is temporary by construction, and every local sunrise is quietly resetting you toward the new meridian.
