## The Basic Shape of the Game

Cricket is a contest between **two teams of eleven** played on a field with a rectangular **pitch** at its centre, 22 yards (about 20 metres) long. At each end of the pitch is a **wicket**: three wooden stumps with two small **bails** resting on top. One team **bats** — trying to score **runs** — while the other **bowls** and fields, trying to stop runs and dismiss the batters. The batting side sends out players two at a time; when ten of the eleven are "out" (or the agreed overs are finished), the teams swap roles. That single alternation — bat, then bowl — repeated across an innings or two, is the whole skeleton of the sport.

## How You Score Runs

A **run** is scored the simplest way possible: the two batters swap ends of the pitch, touching the ground behind the far crease, and that is one run. Hit the ball to the boundary and you skip the running entirely — reaching the rope on the ground scores **4**, clearing it in the air scores **6**. Because a batter keeps scoring only while the pair survive, a long, high innings is really a story of partnerships: two players each not getting out for a long stretch, piling up runs together.

## How You Get Out

Dismissing a batter — taking a **wicket** — is where the variety lives. The common methods:

- **Bowled:** the ball hits the stumps, knocking off a bail.
- **Caught:** a fielder catches the ball after the bat touches it, before it bounces.
- **LBW (leg before wicket):** the ball would have hit the stumps but the batter's leg blocked it.
- **Run out:** a fielder breaks the far wicket with the ball while a batter is stranded between the ends.
- **Stumped:** the wicket-keeper puts down the wicket while a batter has stepped out of their ground and fails to get back.

A bowler's success is measured in wickets; a batter's in runs. The tension of the game is the duel between the one person throwing the ball and the one person trying to hit it.

## Overs: Why the Counting Is Different Here

Bowling is doled out in **overs**: six legal deliveries from one bowler, after which the ball must be bowled from the other end by a different bowler. Limiting *how many* overs each side gets is what creates the three main formats. A batter must therefore balance two instincts — scoring runs *now* and not getting out, because you only ever get one life. That pressure, capped by an overs limit, is why the same set of rules produces three completely different sports.

## The Three Formats

- **Test cricket** is the oldest and longest: no overs cap, each side bats two "innings", and a match runs up to **five days**. Patience and technique over sheer speed define it; drawing is a normal result.
- **One-Day Internationals (ODI)** give each side **50 overs**. Slower than T20, faster than Test, and the format of the marquee **50-over World Cup**.
- **Twenty20 (T20)** is a sprint: **20 overs** a side, big hitting from the first ball, and matches over in about three hours. It turned cricket into a television-friendly, franchise-driven global game.

The mental shift between them is huge: in a Test you can survive an hour without scoring; in a T20 you can lose the match doing the same.

## The Tournament Everyone Watches

Cricket's world stage is the **ICC Cricket World Cup**, contested in the 50-over ODI format every four years — **Australia** holds the most titles, and the sport's heartland runs through the Commonwealth: England, Australia, India, Pakistan, the Caribbean, South Africa and Sri Lanka, with India and the others also dominating the shorter **T20 World Cup**.
