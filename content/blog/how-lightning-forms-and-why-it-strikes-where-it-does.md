TITLE: How Lightning Forms, and Why It Strikes Where It Does
EXCERPT: A single flash carries more current than a power station, heats the air hotter than the sun's surface, and creates the thunder you hear seconds later. Here is the honest physics of how a cloud decides where to discharge — and how to stay safe under one.
TAGS: lightning, thunderstorms, weather, safety, physics, electricity

## The Separation: How a Cloud Builds a Battery

Lightning begins as a problem of sorting. Inside a towering cumulonimbus cloud, updrafts carry supercooled water droplets and ice crystals upward while heavier graupel (soft hail) falls through them. When ice crystals collide with graupel, electrons get scraped off one and left on the other — a contact-charge effect, not so different from rubbing a balloon on your sleeve. The cloud's gravity does the rest: the lighter, positively charged ice crystals get carried to the top of the cloud, while the negatively charged graupel settles in the middle and lower regions. The result is an ordinary capacitor, with the cloud as one plate and the ground below as another.

That is why lightning is overwhelmingly a phenomenon of *deep* clouds. The charging process needs a strong, sustained updraft to keep crystals and graupel colliding for tens of minutes. On [weather balloon sounding data](https://tukuk.org/blog/how-a-weather-forecast-is-made-from-balloon-to-supercomputer), forecasters look for exactly the conditions that build such clouds: warm, moist air near the surface, a steep temperature drop with height (instability), and a cap of warm air aloft that lets energy accumulate before everything breaks open at once.

## The Leader: The Cloud Tests the Air

Air is a good insulator, and the field built up across a kilometre of sky is not enough on its own to punch through it. What happens next is one of the most elegant sequences in nature. Once the field between cloud and ground reaches roughly ten thousand volts per centimetre locally, a faint, stepping channel of ionised air called a **stepped leader** pushes down from the cloud base in jumps of about fifty metres, each taking around fifty microseconds. It is almost invisible, about as wide as a coin, and it forks as it descends, probing several paths at once.

Meanwhile, the approaching negative charge above pushes the positive charge in the ground upward. From tall, pointy objects — trees, masts, church spires, the tip of a kite string — **streamers** rise to meet the descending leader. The object that produces the strongest streamer has effectively been chosen. This is why lightning "strikes where it does": the final path is decided by a competition among hundreds of candidate connection points, and the winner is usually the one whose upward streamer intercepts a downward leader first. The spectacular bolt you see is only the return stroke — a brilliant surge of current racing up the now-completed channel at a third of the speed of light, roughly 30,000 amps for a typical flash, heating the channel to about 30,000 °C.

## Thunder: The Sound of Exploding Air

The heat is so extreme and so sudden that the air in the channel explosively expands, then collapses back when it cools milliseconds later. That double shock becomes an acoustic pulse that we hear as thunder. A lightning channel can be kilometres long, and each point along it is a separate source of sound, so the rumbling that lasts for seconds is the same strike arriving from different distances. Counting the seconds between flash and first thunder and dividing by three gives the distance in kilometres — because light crosses that gap instantly while sound crawls at 343 metres per second. Beyond about fifteen kilometres, the thunder from most flashes dissipates or bends away from the ground entirely, which is why you sometimes see distant lightning in perfect silence. That far-off flicker has its own name — sheet lightning — and it is not a different kind of storm, just one whose thunder never reaches you.

## Cloud-to-Cloud and the Weird Ones

Roughly three-quarters of all lightning never touches the ground at all. Intra-cloud flashes between the negative and positive regions of the same cloud, or between neighbouring clouds, do the real work of neutralising a storm's charge; cloud-to-ground strokes are the minority, just the ones we notice. The ground is not safe from the others either: a strike that enters a tree and exits into the soil can travel along the surface, which is why the standard advice is to stand with feet together in an open area rather than lie flat — spreading your feet apart puts your two ankles at different voltages during a nearby strike.

And there are cousins above the clouds. Transient luminous events — red sprites, blue jets, elves — are now well-documented discharges between the tops of powerful storms and the lower ionosphere, visible from aircraft and satellites for milliseconds at a time. Their existence confirms that the electrical circuit of a thunderstorm reaches much higher than the cloud itself, a reminder that the atmosphere is a globally connected electrical system rather than a collection of local weather events.

## Staying Safe Under a Storm

The rules that actually work are boring: when you hear thunder, you are close enough to be struck, so get inside a substantial building or a metal car (the shell diverts current around you, not through you). Avoid open ground, hilltops, water, and isolated trees — the tree survives the strike, you would not. If caught in the open with no shelter, crouch low on the balls of your feet, heels touching, hands over ears, and spread out from anyone else by at least fifteen metres. The old advice to "lie flat" is dead wrong, and so is the myth that rubber-soled shoes protect you — a lightning bolt has just crossed kilometres of insulating air. The statistics say the overwhelming majority of lightning casualties happen to people who were outside, doing ordinary things, near the first or last thunder of a storm, when the cell was closer than it looked. Thirty minutes after the last rumble is the earliest reasonable all-clear. The [cloud types that build lightning](https://tukuk.org/blog/cloud-types-and-what-they-mean-for-weather) are worth learning to recognise: a dark, anvil-topped cumulonimbus with a boiled-cauliflower base is the storm that will strike, and it tells you so long before the first drop falls.
