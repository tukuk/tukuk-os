TITLE: The Science of Cooking — Heat, Salt, Fat and Water
EXCERPT: Every recipe ever written is applied physics with a taste test. Four forces — heat, salt, fat and water — do almost all the work in a kitchen, and once you can name them, instructions stop being arbitrary and start being obvious. Here is the model behind good cooking.
TAGS: cooking, science, recipes, food, chemistry, nutrition

## Heat: The Reaction Scheduler

Cooking is deliberately starting chemical reactions, and temperature chooses which ones. Below about 100 °C water does the running: starches gelatinise (rice and pasta hydrate and soften), proteins uncoil and set (the clear white of an egg turns opaque at roughly 62–70 °C — why gentle heat makes custard and fierce heat makes scrambled rubber). Above the boiling point, in oils and on a hot pan, the great flavour reactions begin: **caramelisation** is sugar decomposing past about 160 °C, while the **Maillard reaction** — named for the French chemist who described it in 1912 — joins amino acids to sugars at roughly 140–165 °C and manufactures the crust on bread, the sear on a steak and the aroma of roasted coffee. Both need a dry surface, which is the real reason recipes say "pat the meat dry": water clamped at 100 °C is a thermostat that blocks every browning reaction. A pan crowded with wet food steams instead of searing; the physics is the same whether you are cooking for two or two hundred.

## Salt: The Flavour Multiplexer

Salt does far more than taste salty. It draws water out of cells by osmosis (why salted cucumber weeps, and why dry-brining a steak overnight dries its surface *in* the fridge while seasoning deep). It changes proteins: salted water makes ham hold moisture, unsalted cod flakes; salt loosens gluten in bread and tightens egg white. And its deepest trick is suppression — salt at cooking levels quiets bitterness and lets existing aromas read louder, which is why a pinch in sweet coffee or tomato sauce improves it without tasting of anything. The measurable world of food quantities — portions, salts, sugars, what a label's numbers actually mean — is decoded in [how to read a nutrition label](https://tukuk.org/blog/how-to-read-a-nutrition-label) and on the food-analysis pages at [tukuk.org/nutrition](https://tukuk.org/nutrition).

## Fat: The Flavour Delivery System

Flavour molecules are mostly fat-soluble, which is why butter carries garlic and oil carries chilli far better than water does — a broth tastes round, a butter sauce tastes intense. Fat also conducts heat past water's ceiling (frying at 180 °C is only possible in oil), tenderises by coating flour proteins and blocking water (shortcrumb pastry "shortens" because fat literally interrupts gluten), and stays liquid in the cold for the same long-molecule chemistry that separates olive oil from butter. Smoke points differ per fat, so choosing an oil is choosing a temperature: high-heat searing wants refined oils with long saturated or monounsaturated chains; a salad dressing can afford the delicate ones.

## Water: The Medium and the Enemy

Water sets the upper cooking temperature of stews, pasta and steaming at 100 °C (a pressure cooker lifts that ceiling to around 120 °C by raising the pressure over the water), and its removal is preservation: jam, dried fish and jerky all work by leaving microbes nothing to drink. Hard water's minerals change dough and tea; soft water behaves differently; and the ratio of water to grain decides whether rice is fluffy or porridge.

## Why This Makes You a Better Cook

Hold the four and a recipe becomes a set of clues you can interrogate: "high heat, short time" is Maillard; "low and slow" is collagen melting into gelatin; "finish with butter" is aroma delivery; "salt early" is osmosis doing the seasoning for you. The recipe collections at [tukuk.org/recipes](https://tukuk.org/recipes) gather the dishes you can now interrogate this way — every cooking method as a physical operation you can predict, adjust and fix, instead of a ritual to obey. The best cooks are not following instructions. They are running controlled experiments with a very forgiving lab.
