TITLE: What Your Blood Pressure Numbers Actually Mean
EXCERPT: "120 over 80" is one of the most quoted health numbers there is, and almost no one is told what the two figures really measure. Blood pressure is a live reading of the force your heart exerts on your arteries — and understanding the two numbers turns a scary reading into useful information.
TAGS: blood pressure, health, heart, science, everyday health

## Two Numbers, Two Phases of a Beat

Blood pressure is written as two numbers, say **120/80 mmHg** ("millimetres of mercury," the old unit). The **top number is systolic** — the peak pressure in your arteries when the heart muscle contracts and pumps a stroke of blood out into them. The **bottom number is diastolic** — the lowest pressure in those same arteries when the heart relaxes and refills between beats. Your arteries are elastic, so they stretch to absorb the systolic surge and then recoil to keep blood moving during diastole; that is why there is never a moment of zero pressure between beats. Every ~24 hours your heart performs this pressure cycle around 100,000 times.

## It's a Measure of Force on the Vessel Wall

What these numbers actually quantify is the **force of blood pushing against arterial walls**. That force has to be high enough to reach your brain and kidneys, but sustained high pressure is work the heart must over-perform and a strain the vessels must absorb. Pressure is not the same as heart rate (beats per minute) and not the same as blood volume, though all are related — you can have a normal pulse and high pressure, which is exactly why you cannot feel your own blood pressure and need a cuff to know it.

## What "High" Means, and Why It Matters

Medical bodies set the reference lines slightly differently, but the widely used framing is: a normal reading is below about **120/80**, and consistently at or above roughly **140/90** is **hypertension** (some guidelines flag concern from 130/80 upward). Hypertension is called the "silent" problem because it usually has no symptoms until, over years, it quietly raises the risk of **stroke, heart attack, heart failure and kidney damage** by forcing the heart to work harder and damaging artery walls so they narrow and stiffen. Because it is invisible, the only way to manage it is to measure it — a single high reading isn't a diagnosis, since pressure fluctuates through the day, which is why clinicians check it repeatedly over time.

## What Moves the Numbers

Blood pressure responds to both short-term and long-term factors. It naturally rises with activity, stress and caffeine, and falls with sleep and relaxation. Long-term, the biggest everyday levers are **salt** (which holds extra water and raises volume), **body weight**, **physical activity**, **alcohol** and a diet low in potassium-rich foods. Genetics and age matter too — vessels stiffen with time, nudging the systolic number up. Many of the same controllable factors overlap with other common risk measures, which is why [understanding what your BMI means](https://tukuk.org/blog/bmi-explained-what-it-measures-and-where-it-fails) and learning to [read a nutrition label](https://tukuk.org/blog/how-to-read-a-nutrition-label) to see your salt and sugar intake sit in the same picture as the blood-pressure cuff.

## Reading a Cuff Correctly

If you check your own blood pressure, small habits decide whether the number is trustworthy. Rest quietly for a few minutes, sit with feet flat and the arm supported at heart level, use a cuff sized for your upper arm, and take two readings a minute apart rather than reacting to one. Track the average over several days instead of fixating on a single reading, and bring that log — not just a memory of the highest number you ever saw — to your clinician. Blood pressure is one of the few genuinely life-saving numbers you can measure yourself cheaply and often, and knowing what the top and bottom figures mean makes it far less mysterious the next time the cuff squeezes.
