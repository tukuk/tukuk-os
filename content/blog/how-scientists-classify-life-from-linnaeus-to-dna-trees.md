## The Address System: Names That Are Also Claims

Carl Linnaeus gave biology its operating procedure in the 1750s: every species gets a two-part Latin name — *Homo sapiens*, *Panthera tigris* — a genus plus a specific epithet, and names follow strict rules of priority (the first validly published name wins, which is why some charming names are legally locked in). Beneath the name sits a ranked ladder: kingdom, phylum, class, order, family, genus, species — memorised by generations of students. But the deep idea was never ranking; it was **grouping by shared characteristics**, which after Darwin gained a mechanism: groups should reflect common descent. A classification became a hypothesis about ancestry, testable and falsifiable like any other science.

## The Cladistic Revolution: Only Branches Count

From the 1960s, Willi Hennig's method — cladistics — changed the game: build trees only from shared derived traits, and reject any group that excludes some descendants. Birds had to count as reptiles (they are descended from dinosaurs); "fish" that excludes tetrapods is an artificial bucket; and reptile-without-birds became invalid no matter how the skin feels. Then DNA sequencing turned the argument from opinion into measurement: comparing conserved genes let systematists count differences and date splits. The biggest shock came in 1977, when Carl Woese read the RNA of "bacteria" and found **Archaea** — a second lineage as distinct from bacteria as bacteria are from us, hiding in swamps and hot springs and your gut. Three domains replaced two kingdoms; fungi were confirmed as our cousins, not plants' neighbours; and the tree gained grafts, because lateral gene transfer shuffles genes between bacterial branches like a family tree with scandal in it.

## The Count That Is Also a Guess

Scientists have formally described around two million species; honest estimates of the total range from eight to ten million for eukaryotes — with insects, fungi and deep-sea and soil microbiota accounting for most of the blank space. Naming is not a stamp-collecting exercise: you cannot protect, farm or study the medicine in a species you haven't defined, and new species are still found every month, including large mammals in the 21st century. Modern discovery is increasingly collaborative and digital: photographs identified by communities feed distribution maps, the process behind [how scientists know where animals are](https://tukuk.org/blog/how-scientists-know-where-animals-are-the-community-science-answer), and barcoding — matching a short standard gene stretch — can tell a fish market whether its "cod" is actually cod.

## Where You Can Walk the Tree

The living-index pages at [tukuk.org/life](https://tukuk.org/life) catalogue species with their classification, distribution and conservation status — every entry carrying its address in the same system Linnaeus started and genomics rewrote. Use it like a field naturalist: pick an animal, walk up its ladder (species → genus → family → order), and notice what each rank now *claims* about ancestry. Classification is biology's oldest argument and its most productive one — every dispute about moving a branch is a sign that someone found better data. The tree of life is not finished; it is being redrawn continuously, by people with sequencers instead of quills, in the same spirit of the original project: give life its addresses, and the map of the living world starts making sense.
