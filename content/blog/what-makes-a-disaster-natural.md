TITLE: What Makes a Disaster "Natural"? The Equation Behind Every Headline
EXCERPT: Earthquakes do not kill people; buildings do. Floods do not drown cities; houses in the wrong place do. Disaster science has carried one deceptively simple formula since the 1970s — hazard times exposure times vulnerability — and using it changes how every headline about catastrophe should be read.
TAGS: disasters, risk, earthquake, flood, climate, vulnerability

## The Formula That Rewrites the Headline

The core claim of disaster research is arithmetic: **Risk = Hazard × Exposure × Vulnerability**. A magnitude-8 earthquake under an uninhabited desert is a geological event with zero disaster. The same quake under Dhaka is an emergency. A category-4 hurricane hitting an empty coastline damages trees; the same storm over New Orleans tests a century of levee decisions. Nothing about the hazard changed — the disaster lives in the other two terms, and both of those are human choices: *exposure* is where we allow people to live and work; *vulnerability* is how badly those places fail when tested. The live open events on [tukuk.org/disasters](https://tukuk.org/disasters) let you test the equation directly: the same volcanic eruption or flood, with wildly different human consequences depending entirely on the two terms on the right.

## Exposure Is the Fastest-Growing Term

Hazards have not multiplied; exposure has. The world's megacities cluster exactly where the formula punishes them: deltas (Jakarta, Dhaka, Lagos, Bangkok — subsiding and flooded), coasts (Miami, Manila), fault zones (Tehran, Istanbul, Tokyo), drylands on the wildland edge. Hundreds of millions more people now stand in harm's way than in 1960, and property per person is worth far more, so identical physical events produce exponentially larger loss figures. That is the honest reason disaster *cost* trends upward while hazard frequency mostly does not — a distinction worth holding onto every time a headline claims the sky is falling more often.

## Vulnerability Is the Term You Can Legislate

The difference between Christchurch and Port-au-Prince was not the earthquakes; it was building codes, enforcement, and poverty. Vulnerability is the measurable part of the equation: how houses are built, whether warnings are heeded and reachable, whether the poorest live on the slopes and floodplains no one else wanted, whether a government can evacuate a city or only pray. It is also the part with proof of concept: Bangladesh's cyclone death toll fell from hundreds of thousands in 1970 to hundreds in recent decades — not fewer storms, but shelters, warning chains and the community systems behind [how river flood forecasts work](https://tukuk.org/blog/how-river-flood-forecasts-work-and-what-the-number-means) and [how tsunami warnings work](https://tukuk.org/blog/what-causes-a-tsunami-and-how-warnings-work). The geology behind the hazards — why the quakes, storms and eruptions happen where they do — is mapped in [how volcanoes form](https://tukuk.org/blog/how-volcanoes-form-and-why-they-erupt) and [how earthquakes are measured](https://tukuk.org/blog/how-earthquakes-are-measured-magnitude-versus-intensity).

## Why "Natural" Is Now in Quotes

The consequence of the formula: calling floods, quakes and heatwaves "natural disasters" quietly blames weather for what zoning, budgets and corruption did. Researchers increasingly just say *disaster*, full stop, because the hazard is only the first third. This is not word-play — it changes who is accountable. A hazard is geography; a disaster is a decision. The next time the news says "nature struck," run the three terms: what was the hazard, who was exposed, and what made them breakable? Three honest answers will usually name the real cause — and that cause is almost never the earthquake.
