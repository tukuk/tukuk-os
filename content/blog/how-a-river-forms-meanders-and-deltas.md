EXCERPT: Every river is a machine for moving water and sediment downhill, and that one job carves its whole personality — the V-valley near its source, the snake-like bends in its middle, the fan of channels where it meets the sea.
TAGS: rivers, geography, geology, deltas, science

## One Job: Gravity, Water and Sediment

A river begins as rain and snowmelt that, instead of evaporating or soaking in, runs downhill into a channel. Gravity is the engine, and the river spends its entire life doing two things at once: **eroding** (cutting into the ground and picking up material) and **transporting** that sediment downstream. What a river looks like at any point is just the balance between how much energy it has and how much rough material it is carrying.

Rivers are usually described in three reaches — upper, middle and lower — and each has its own signature.

## The Upper Course: Cutting Down

Near the **source** (or headwaters), often in high country, the water is steep, fast and full of energy directed *downward*. It cuts deep into the rock, and because the sides are not yet undercut much, it leaves a steep-sided **V-shaped valley**. The bed is rough with boulders, the flow is turbulent, and you get waterfalls and rapids where the water drops over resistant rock. Here erosion is dominated by **vertical** cutting — the river is busy lowering its own bed.

## The Middle Course: Side-to-Side and Meanders

As the gradient eases, the river has less downward energy and starts using it **sideways** — and that is where the beautiful bends appear. A tiny asymmetry in a flow gets amplified: faster water on the **outside** of a bend erodes that bank (cut bank), while slower water on the **inside** drops its load of sand and gravel (point bar). The bend therefore migrates outward and downstream over time, growing into a **meander**, a great sinuous loop.

Meanders do not stay polite. As loops grow they can pinch toward each other, and during a flood the river may cut straight across the narrow "neck," abandoning the old loop. The cut-off bend, stranded on the floodplain, slowly fills and becomes an **oxbow lake**. This is why a walk along a mature river valley is dotted with crescent-shaped lakes that are the ghosts of former meanders. The valley here is a wide **floodplain**, built from the sediment the river spreads when it overflows.

## The Lower Course: Losing Energy, Dropping Load

Near sea level the river is carrying a huge volume of water but has almost no gradient left, so it is slow and heavily laden with fine silt. It meanders grandly, its floodplain broad, and it is now mostly a **depositional** system. Everything the river picked up upstream is on its way to the sea, and the moment the flow slows, it starts unloading.

## The Delta: Where the River Ends

When a river reaches a standing body of water — a sea or a lake — its forward speed collapses, and it dumps its sediment right at the mouth. The deposited material obstructs the flow, forcing the single channel to split into many smaller **distributary** channels that braid across the growing pile of sediment: this fan of land and channels is a **delta**. Deltas are extraordinarily fertile (built from all that upstream silt), which is why river mouths have cradled civilisations — the Nile, the Ganges–Brahmaputra, the Mississippi. A river that drops more sediment than the sea can wash away builds land outward; a river starved of sediment (by dams upstream, for instance) is eroded back by the coast.

Rivers are not fixed lines on a map. They are slow, patient machines that carve valleys, wander sideways across floodplains and build new land where they finally meet the sea.
