TITLE: Why Drizzle Is Not Just Light Rain: The Physics of Raindrops
EXCERPT: Drizzle, rain, and downpour are not the same process at different volumes — they come from different clouds, different droplet growth mechanisms, and produce drops of different sizes. Here is why the sky has more than one way to make water fall.
TAGS: rain, drizzle, drops, weather, clouds, physics

## Two Ways to Grow a Drop

A cloud is, to begin with, a suspension of microscopic droplets — around twenty micrometres across — so light that updrafts carry them indefinitely. To make rain, the cloud has to grow those specks a thousandfold in volume, and it has two fundamentally different manufacturing lines. The **collision-coalescence** process works in warm clouds: droplets fall at different speeds according to size, so big ones overtake small ones, absorb them, and run away with the collected mass in a chain reaction. The **ice-crystal** (Bergeron–Findeisen) process works where cloud tops freeze: ice crystals grow by stealing vapour from supercooled droplets, because air holds less moisture over ice than over water, and the crystals fall as snow that melts into rain on the way down. Clouds use one or both, and which line a cloud used leaves a fingerprint on the drops that reach your window.

That fingerprint is the **drop-size distribution**, and it is the real difference between drizzle and rain. Drizzle drops are tiny — ten to one hundred micrometres — and come mainly from the warm, gentle updrafts of stratus and nimbostratus decks, fed by countless small droplets that never had the space or time to collide their way upward in size. Rain drops, typically half a millimetre to five millimetres, are the product of the growth race inside deeper clouds with strong vertical motion — the same cumuliform engines described in [how lightning forms](https://tukuk.org/blog/how-lightning-forms-and-why-it-strikes-where-it-does). So drizzle is not "weak rain". It is a different size class, delivered by a different process, from a different kind of cloud.

## Why Big Drops Do Not Exist

A persistent childhood theory holds that bigger raindrops are more teardrop-shaped, like the falling-drop icon on weather apps. In reality, drops above about a millimetre flatten into hamburger buns as air resistance squashes their bottoms, and near five millimetres they become unstable parachutes of water that burst apart into smaller drops. That is why you will essentially never see a naturally falling drop the size of a coin: the physics of surface tension versus airflow caps rain at a maximum stable size. (The coin-sized splashes on your sleeve are single drops that already broke up mid-air and landed together.) Drizzle, being far below the limit, stays spherical — which is also precisely why it behaves so differently once it is falling.

## The Fall: Why Drizzle Never Gets Wet All at Once

Terminal velocity scales badly with size. A drizzle drop falls at a few tens of centimetres per second and is nearly at the mercy of the air: it drifts, hangs in the updraft, and takes minutes to descend from a low cloud. A five-millimetre raindrop plummets at eight or nine metres per second, punching through updrafts and reaching the ground in under a minute from cloud base. That difference explains the *experience* of each: rain falls in a sheet you can feel immediately, while drizzle — "mist", "fine spray", the damp that soaks a jacket over an hour — is a slow diffusion of water that only wets you because the journey down was sideways as much as vertical. Drizzle from a hill-cloud can even fall upward in an updraft. And when freezing drizzle meets a sub-zero surface, its tiny supercooled drops flash-freeze on contact into glaze ice — far more dangerous per millimetre than snow, because it spreads as a clear, smooth film over roads and power lines.

## Reading the Ground for the Process Overhead

You can diagnose which cloud factory ran just by watching what lands. Uniform, slow-drifting specks that never form puddles quickly mean drizzle from a shallow stratiform deck. Drops of many sizes falling at once, splashing on hard ground instantly, mean a deep cloud with active collision growth. Large, widely spaced drops that darken paving one at a time mean the storm's core is close and the updraft was strong enough to carry water high before letting it go — often the first heavy edge of a convective cell. Meteorologists measure the same thing professionally with disdrometers and radar reflectivity, because drop size, not just water volume, controls everything from soil erosion (big drops detach soil particles; drizzle barely disturbs them) to how rainfall feeds reservoirs versus how much drifts away unabsorbed. [Reading a weather map](https://tukuk.org/blog/how-to-read-a-weather-map-pressure-fronts-and-clouds) tells you which cloud deck is arriving; the look of the first drops confirms what the deck is made of. The next time the sky offers its two versions of "wet", you will know they are different sentences, not different volumes of the same one.
