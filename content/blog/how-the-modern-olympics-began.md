## A Games Older Than the Idea of "Sport"

Athletic competition at **Olympia** in Greece is traditionally dated to **776 BC**, and for nearly twelve centuries the games were the centre of a religious festival honouring Zeus. Only freeborn Greek men competed; winners took home not money or medals but a wreath of olive and, back home, lasting honour — some were even treated as heroes. The ancient games were brutal and sacred at once, and they kept their four-year rhythm, the **Olympiad**, so faithfully that Greeks counted time by it.

Then it ended. In **393 AD** the Roman emperor Theodosius I banned pagan festivals as Christianity spread, and Olympia fell silent. The Olympics vanished from the calendar for roughly fifteen hundred years — long enough that their return in the modern era was not a continuation but an act of deliberate imagination.

## One Man Decides to Resurrect Them

The modern Olympics are the project of a single enthusiast: **Pierre de Coubertin**, a French educator who believed organised international sport could build peace and character better than words could. In 1894 he convened a congress in Paris and founded the **International Olympic Committee (IOC)**, and in **1896** the first modern Games opened in **Athens**, deliberately held in the country of their origin. Fourteen nations sent about 240 athletes, all men, competing in athletics, cycling, fencing, gymnastics, shooting, swimming, tennis, weightlifting and wrestling. The experiment worked: a Games every four years, an Olympiad revived, was born.

Coubertin's framing shaped everything we still say about the Olympics — that taking part matters more than winning, that the Games are about participation of nations rather than individuals chasing prize money. Whether the ancient games were really so idealistic is debated by historians, but the modern myth took hold and has never quite let go.

## The Symbols That Made It Feel Ancient

The modern Games invented traditions to look older than they were. The **Olympic flag with five interlocking rings** was designed by Coubertin around 1913 and first flown in 1920. The rings and their colours — blue, yellow, black, green and red on a white field — were chosen because every nation's flag contains at least one of them, and the five rings stand for the union of the inhabited continents. The **Olympic flame**, lit at Olympia by mirror and carried by relay, sounds immemorial but was introduced only in **1928**, and the torch relay itself is a far more recent (and, frankly, loaded) invention of 1936.

## Summer, Then Winter

For the first decades there was only the Summer Games. **Winter** sports crept in as demonstrations, then got their own edition in **1924** at Chamonix, France — later retroactively recognised as the first Winter Olympics. Splitting the seasons gave snow and ice sports their own identity and, eventually, a separate four-year cycle that now alternates with the summer games so that *some* Olympics happen every two years.

## Four Years, With Exceptions

The Olympiad rhythm has held with grim consistency — interrupted only by world war. The **1916** Games (Berlin), **1940** and **1944** editions were **cancelled entirely**, never postponed: the medals were simply never awarded, a reminder that the Games are scheduled on a human calendar that war can wipe out. When they resumed in **London 1948**, austerity defined them; when they returned to **Tokyo in 1964**, satellite television made them a genuinely global spectacle for the first time, and the modern era of the Olympics as the planet's biggest stage began.

Today the rotation of host cities is itself a story of politics, money and architecture — every edition leaves a host city, a roster of champions and a set of records behind it.
