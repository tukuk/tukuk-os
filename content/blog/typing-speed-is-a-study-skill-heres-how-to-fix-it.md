TITLE: Typing Speed Is a Study Skill — Here Is How to Fix It in Ten Minutes a Day
EXCERPT: Every keystroke you have to think about is a thought you cannot spend on the sentence. Research on writing keeps finding the same threshold — and the drill to cross it takes one browser tab and a week of short laps.
TAGS: learning, study skills, typing, writing, keyboard, tools

## The bottleneck between your brain and the page

Ask a student why their essay is thin and you will hear about ideas. Ask why their *notes* are thin and the honest answer is often mechanical: the fingers cannot keep up with the sentence, so the sentence gets shortened, dropped, smoothed over. Transcription — getting thought into text — is a motor skill, and when it is slow it taxes working memory, which is exactly the account the composing itself needs to draw from.

Research on writing keeps finding the same shape: up to roughly fifteen to twenty words a minute, the speed *is* the constraint — fluency, sentence length and even idea quality improve as typing gets faster. Past that threshold the fingers stop being the bottleneck and the writing problems become real writing problems. So for a large number of students, the cheapest upgrade available to their essays is not another planning technique. It is typing without looking, accurately, at twenty words a minute.

## Measure the number before you train it

You need one baseline, and you do not need software for it. Open a [typing speed test](https://tukuk.org/alat/typing-test), type the sentence shown — the timer starts at your first keystroke and stops when the sentence matches — and read the two numbers it gives you: words per minute and accuracy, checked word by word as you go. The tool counts a word the honest way, five correct *characters* to a word, so the speed cannot be inflated by bursts of typos.

Below fifteen wpm, the keyboard is part of your homework load. Between fifteen and forty, you are functional and improvable. The number that matters most is not the first one: **accuracy above ninety-five per cent first**, because every error you build in is one you practise, and speed on top of sloppy habits is speed you will have to unlearn.

## The drill: short, timed, daily

Typing improves the way all motor skill does — spaced, not massed. Ten minutes a day beats a Saturday hour, and the drills on the page slot into a study routine as a warm-up rather than a subject: two or three laps before the first study session (the [companion guide to planning a study week](https://tukuk.org/blog/how-to-plan-a-study-week-that-survives-real-life) puts them at the foot of the grid), one lap before a typed essay as a finger-loosener, one lap on the dull days.

Three rules do most of the training on their own. **Never look at the keys** — the home row is the anchor and glance-downs are the habit being broken; a [keyboard tester](https://tukuk.org/alat/kbd) shows you which keys you are actually hitting. **Slow down to speed up**: when accuracy dips below ninety-five, the previous minute was too fast for the current skill, and the practice is cementing errors. **Type study material, not nursery rows**: once the home row is comfortable, the best practice text is tomorrow's chapter summary, because the fingers are then rehearsing the vocabulary you will need on the page.

## What the number buys back

The payoff is not typing faster in a test; it is what the freed attention spends itself on. Notes that keep up with the lecture become full sentences you can actually revise from — the retrieval practice in [active recall](https://tukuk.org/blog/active-recall-why-testing-yourself-beats-re-reading) only works if there is something recorded to recall from. Timed, typed exam answers stop leaving ideas unsaid because the clock ran out mid-paragraph. And the essay written at twenty-five clean words a minute is edited rather than survived, because by the third paragraph the keyboard has stopped being a topic of conversation.

Ten minutes a day, accuracy before speed, real material under the fingers: that is the whole programme. The baseline takes sixty seconds to find — the tab is already open.
