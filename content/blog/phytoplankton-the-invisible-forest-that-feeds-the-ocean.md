## The Base of the Marine Pyramid

Nearly all animal life in the ocean, from anchovies to whales, ultimately runs on a class of organisms too small to see: **phytoplankton** — "drifting plants" in Greek, though most are not plants in the strict sense but protists and cyanobacteria suspended in the sunlit top hundred metres of water. The cast includes **diatoms**, glass-shelled algae that in bloom can turn coastal water the colour of brown paint; **dinoflagellates**, many armored and some responsible for red tides and paralytic shellfish poisoning; coccolithophores, unicellular algae sheathed in calcium-carbonate plates so fine that chalk cliffs like Dover are their accumulated fossils; and the cyanobacteria *Prochlorococcus* and *Synechococcus*, the most abundant photosynthetic organisms on the planet — a liter of open-ocean water may hold a hundred billion *Prochlorococcus* cells, and their total population is counted in the octillions. Though individually microscopic, collectively they fix carbon through photosynthesis at a rate comparable to all the land forests of the world combined: the ocean accounts for roughly half of global primary production, and in doing so they generate a large share of the oxygen exchanged between the biosphere and the air — the popular "every second breath" phrasing overstates it slightly because most marine oxygen is consumed by ocean life itself, but the scale is real, and the [chemistry of how light powers that reaction](https://tukuk.org/blog/how-photosynthesis-works-the-reaction-that-powers-most-life) is identical in a cell and a leaf.

## Blooms, Limits, and the Season

Phytoplankton need light, carbon dioxide, water — and nutrients, chiefly nitrate, phosphate, and the trace metal iron, and it is the nutrients that set the map. Open ocean subtropical gyres, warmed and stratified year-round, are the biological deserts of the sea: clear blue water means nothing is growing in it. Fertility appears where the deep ocean's stockpile of unused nutrients is dragged back into the light — along the wind-driven [upwelling currents](https://tukuk.org/blog/how-ocean-currents-work-the-global-conveyor-belt) of the Peru and Benguela systems, in the storm-mixed coastal zones, and in the high latitudes where spring returns sunlight to nutrient-rich winter-mixed water. Those seasonal explosions — visible from space as swirls of turquoise and milky green, mapped continuously by satellite ocean-colour instruments — feed the zooplankton, which feed the fish larvae, which feed everything with fins, and the timing is critical: a spring bloom that arrives out of sync with the life cycle of the copepods that eat it can ripple upward through the whole food web. Iron limits growth even where nitrate is plentiful — the "high-nutrient, low-chlorophyll" regions around Antarctica and in the equatorial Pacific — a fact that inspired the disputed geoengineering idea of iron fertilisation, of which more below.

## The Ocean's Carbon Elevator

Phytoplankton's most consequential trick happens after they die. Carbon fixed at the surface leaves the atmosphere in two ways: as **marine snow** — aggregates of dead cells, pellets and mucus that sink, slowly decomposing on the way down and releasing their carbon back into deep water — and in the shells of the dead: diatom glass and coccolith chalk fall to the seafloor, and the carbon locked in the sediments is stored on geological timescales. The biological carbon pump moves the equivalent of many billions of tonnes of CO₂ from contact with the air into the ocean's interior each year, and it is a major reason the atmosphere is as cool as it is; the deep sea holds dozens of times more carbon than the air above it. When pump efficiency changed in past climate transitions, atmospheric CO₂ changed with it, and sediment cores carry the plankton receipts: foraminifera shells, alkenone biomarkers, and chalk strata that let geochemists reconstruct ancient bloom patterns and ice-age CO₂ from the same centimetre of mud. It is also the reason the ocean has absorbed roughly a quarter of human emissions more or less liveably: the carbon is being pumped down, but dissolving CO₂ also makes seawater more acidic, and the chemistry that corrodes the shells of cold-water snails and threatens the limestone architecture of reefs is the same chemistry, running in reverse.

## What Satellites Now Watch — and What Climate May Change

Since the 1990s, continuous satellite records have let oceanographers track the phytoplankton standing crop of the entire planet, and the honest reading of that record is more careful than the alarmist or triumphalist versions: results depend on the algorithm, the sensor and the baseline, and the main picture is strong regional change without a clean, globally uniform decline — the subtropical gyres appear to have become measurably more barren as warming stratifies the surface and nutrients stay locked below, while some high-latitude regions show greening as ice retreats and the growing season lengthens. Stratification is the mechanism to remember: warm water floating on cold water mixes less, and less mixing means less fertilisation of the sunlit layer. Small shifts matter enormously because the entire open-ocean food web is a thin film of life over abyssal emptiness — a ten-per-cent change in the base is a ten-per-cent change in the ocean's capacity to feed us, and the fishery economics of a billion people ride on it. There is an ethical footnote on iron fertilisation: small experimental dumps of iron did spark blooms, but the carbon they buried proved far less than models hoped, the side effects on oxygen and algae toxins were real, and international moratoria now treat large-scale ocean experiments as off-limits. Phytoplankton are the least famous keystone species on Earth — no photographs, no campaign mascots, just cells drifting in the top few metres — and they hold up the ocean's food, the planet's oxygen budget, and the climate's most important carbon sink at the same time.
