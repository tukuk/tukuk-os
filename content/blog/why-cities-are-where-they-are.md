## The Site Was Chosen Once, and Then Kept

Cities almost never move. The logic of their location was set when the place was decided, often three thousand years before the first photograph of it existed. The world's great cities cluster around a handful of repeatable geographic opportunities:

- **Confluences** — where two rivers meet, one stop controls all the trade of both. Paris sits where the Seine crosses a route from the Rhône to the Atlantic; Belgrade at the Danube-Sava junction; Khartoum at the Blue and White Nile; Chongqing and Wuhan on the Yangtze and its tributaries.
- **Fords and bridges** — the pinch point of a river crossing. Oxford's name literally means "the ford of the oxen"; Frankfurt is "the ford of the Franks"; Cambridge grew at the bridge on the Cam.
- **Estuaries and natural harbours** — where the sea reaches inland with deep water and shelter: London on the Thames tidal basin, Sydney and Rio behind their heads, New York behind the Narrows, Rotterdam at the Rhine mouth.
- **Passes and crossroads** — mountain gaps and caravan routes concentrate traffic into one place: Kabul, Tehran, Samarkand, Denver.
- **Springs, cisterns and defense** — water that has to be shared, ground that has to be held: Jerusalem, Damascus ("the watered land"), and hundreds of hilltop old towns across Europe and the Caucasus.

You can test the pattern yourself: open any city page at [tukuk.org/cities](https://tukuk.org/cities), find its coordinates on the [latitude and longitude primer](https://tukuk.org/blog/latitude-longitude-explained), and trace the terrain. The reason is usually visible in one glance — a bend, a mouth, a gap.

## Once Chosen, the City Keeps Its Own Reasons

Location has gravity. A medieval ford becomes a bridge, the bridge district becomes the financial quarter, the rail terminal is placed next to the old river port, and a 21st-century tech campus is built "near downtown" — meaning near a decision made in 1250. Path dependence is why the centre of London, Istanbul or Mexico City is still the oldest part, and why expansion rings outward from a nucleus nobody would choose today. It also explains the strangenesses: why some capitals sit far from the sea (landlocked logistics hurt), why river crossings still create twin cities (Kuala Lumpur began at the muddy confluence of the Klang and Gombak rivers — its name says so; Buda and Pest on opposite banks), and why harbor cities keep port-adjacent names long after the cranes moved (Piraeus, Yokohama, Liverpool's docks).

## What Changes the Map Anyway

Technology can rewrite a location's fortune without moving anything. Rail made inland junction towns (Wolverhampton, Atlanta — literally a rail crossing) out of nowhere; the container ship dethroned old deep-water docks that were too cramped and elevated Rotterdam, Tanjung Pelepas and Tangier Med; air cargo and highways created the Dallas-Atlanta-Frankfurt airport cities; and now cheap energy and climate decide where the data-center boom towns will be. Rivers still anchor the old cities, but the new ones are chosen by grids and runways.

## Why This Matters for Reading the World

Population futures are written in the site logic of cities: delta megacities sit exactly where floods and storm surge will bite hardest, island ports sit where sea level rise is measured first, and desert cities built on one aquifer or river (Las Vegas, Cairo, Riyadh) show their limits on a map if you know where to look. The river landforms underneath them are explained in [how a river forms, meanders and deltas](https://tukuk.org/blog/how-a-river-forms-meanders-and-deltas), and the pure extremes of settlement — places that cannot be driven to, towns that exist against every geographic rule — are collected at [tukuk.org/records](https://tukuk.org/records). Next time you see a skyline, read it as an argument about geography that some long-dead founder won.
