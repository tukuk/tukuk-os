## The Other Map of the World

There are two honest atlases of Earth. One counts borders, rivers and roads as governments draw them. The other is built from **light**, and it refuses to lie: at night, a city cannot hide, a blackout cannot be spun, and a border lit on one side and dark on the other says more than any summit communiqué. Since the mid-1990s — when defense weather satellites turned their night-vision lenses Earthward — this second atlas has been quietly revised every single night by the **VIIRS day/night band** aboard the Suomi NPP and NOAA-20 satellites, and the pages of the revision are free for anyone to read.

The sensor's trick is sensitivity: it detects photons in the near-infrared, thousands of times fainter than daylight, enough to separate sodium-orange street lamps from white LEDs, and even to image clouds by **moonshine**. A full moon is, apparently, bright enough to see the ground from 824 kilometres up — night imagery is a relay between the sky's two lamps.

## What the Lights Say

- **Development has a glow.** The single most predictive satellite signal for a region's wealth is the total light it emits at night; electrification surveys literally map progress by watching darkness retreat. Whole chapters of economic history are legible: post-war Europe steady, the Gulf's sudden incandescence, China's coast blazing outward along the Yangtze while the interior caught up decade by decade.
- **Borders are bright lines.** The India–Pakistan frontier glows on one side and dims on the other; the Korean DMZ is the darkest strip between Seoul and Pyongyang; at Diwali, India's night-time brightness spikes visibly enough for researchers to track the festival from space — culture registering on an orbital sensor.
- **Disasters flicker.** Wars, earthquakes and hurricanes show up as *absences*: the blackout footprint of a struck grid, a city whose lights fail and return block by block as repairs crawl. Emergency agencies genuinely use night-light change to triage where the power went out.
- **We are losing the night.** Growth in sky brightness — light pollution — doubles roughly every two decades in hot regions, and it is measurable from orbit as well as from the ground: about a third of humanity no longer sees the Milky Way where they sleep. The lights that tell us how we live also tell us what we have dimmed out.

## Reading It Yourself, Tonight

Open [Live Earth](/peta/langit) and choose the **Night lights** layer — it is the same day/night band NASA publishes, renewed about every 24 hours, so you are looking at the planet's most recent sleepless night. Then run the classic exercises: fly along the US east corridor and watch Philadelphia–Baltimore–DC merge into one glow; compare Java's volcano-strung necklace of towns with Kalimantan's dark half of the island; find the Nile — one river of light in a sea of desert, every city exactly where the water allows. Switch back to true-color over the same spot and watch the human map turn into the physical one: harbours where rivers meet sea, oases where caravans stopped, cities where mountains left a gap. The [full story of how this imagery is produced and published free](/blog/see-earth-from-space-today-free-satellite-imagery) pairs with it — and if you want to plan a route through the daylight version of the same world, [Maps & Navigation](/peta) carries both passes as base layers.

## The Fine Print (There Is Some)

Night imagery flatters and omits like any portrait. Cloud cover hides; snow and river valleys gleam misleadingly; LEDs scatter more blue light that sensors register differently from the sodium glow of 1990s cities, so year-over-year "brightness growth" partly measures lamp technology. And the satellite sees *lights*, not lives — a well-lit industrial park is not a neighbourhood. The honest reading is comparative: same sensor, same moon phase, two places — then the glow really does mean something.

Darkness, like light, is data. The planet keeps a night ledger, and the page is never more than one layer-switch away.
