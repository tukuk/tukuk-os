TITLE: How to Look at a Painting — Five Things Your Eye Already Knows but Never Was Taught
EXCERPT: Standing in front of a painting can feel like standing in front of a locked door: everyone says it is beautiful, nobody says what they are actually doing with their eyes. Looking at paintings is a learnable skill made of five ordinary operations — and once you run them deliberately, almost no picture stays flat.
TAGS: art, painting, composition, color, museums, seeing

## One: Find Where Your Eye Is Forced to Go

Every competent painting is a routing plan for your attention. The artist cannot move you like a film can, so they build a path: the sharpest edge, the brightest light, or the only face in the frame pulls you first; then a gesture, a beam, a road nudges you to the second point; the rest waits for the end of the tour. Test any painting at [tukuk.org/art](https://tukuk.org/art) with the squint test — half-close your eyes until detail melts. The shapes that survive are the real structure. If your eye bounces out of the canvas at a corner, or stalls on nothing, that is not your failure — that is the routing failing.

## Two: Notice What Is Placed Against What

Composition is relationships, not objects. A small figure in a huge landscape is a statement about scale; two faces at the same height argue; a vertical door cutting a horizontal table freezes motion. The oldest trick is the triangle: three linked elements form a stable circuit your eye circles for free, which is why Renaissance altarpieces and 1950s ad layouts share the same skeleton. Contrast works the same way — light against dark (chiaroscuro), smooth against rough, warm against cool. Caravaggio built entire scenes from one light source and one shadow wall; Vermeer from the quiet contrast of a lit window against an interior.

## Three: Read Colour as Temperature and Money

Colour has two jobs: temperature and cost. Warm advances, cool recedes — that is how a flat canvas gets depth without perspective lines, and why sunsets read as distance. The economic half is real: for most of history, the expensive pigments signalled the subject's importance. Ultramarine, ground from lapis lazuli traded from Afghanistan, cost more than gold leaf, which is why the Virgin wears blue: the patron's money *is* the composition. Chrome yellow, invented in 1809, made Van Gogh's sunflowers chemically possible; synthetic pigments in 1856 freed colour from mineral geography altogether.

## Four: Ask What the Brush Is Saying About Time

Zoom to a hand, a sleeve, a sky. Tight invisible brushwork (Vermeer, Ingres) hides its making — the picture claims to be a window. Loose, visible strokes (Rembrandt's late years, Soutine, de Kooning) admit a human made this in an hour — the picture claims to be an event. Neither is more skilled; they are different statements about time, and recognising the claim is half of what "style" means. Once you see strokes as decisions, you can watch a painter's confidence — or doubt — directly on the surface.

## Five: Name One Thing You Would Move

The last operation turns looking into judgement: find the one element you would reposition, brighten or remove. Art history is not a museum of agreement — it is a record of exactly that sentence spoken about a masterpiece ("too much blue," "the dog is irrelevant"). Doing it honestly on a great painting is the same discipline the open-access collections invite when you [reuse museum images](https://tukuk.org/blog/what-open-access-museum-art-mean-and-how-to-use-it) and the same narrative craft you can watch in motion when [films borrow the painter's tools of composition](https://tukuk.org/blog/how-films-tell-stories-the-hidden-grammar-of-editing). Five operations, one squint, zero prerequisites — the locked door was never locked; it just needed a handle, and now you have five.
