## The Recipe for a Light Show

An aurora needs three ingredients, and Earth supplies two of them permanently:

1. **A stream of charged particles from the Sun** — the solar wind, a thin flow of electrons and protons that always rushes past Earth at several hundred km/s, and occasionally surges after a solar flare or a coronal mass ejection.
2. **A planetary magnetic field** — Earth's magnetosphere deflects most of the wind, but near the poles the field lines dip down and funnel particles into the upper atmosphere.
3. **Air to collide with.** The particles crash into oxygen and nitrogen roughly 90 to 300 km up, and each collision makes an atom flash — the same physics as the glowing gas in a neon tube, scaled up to a curtain hundreds of kilometres long.

The result is the **auroral oval**, a ring of glowing sky encircling each magnetic pole. In quiet times it sits far north (or south); during storms it expands, and that expansion — not the storm's raw power — decides which cities get a show.

## Why the Colours Differ

- **Green** (the classic) comes from oxygen at around 100 km — the most common colour because that altitude is dense enough for frequent collisions.
- **Red** comes from the same oxygen atom, but higher up (200 km and above) where the rarer red emission has time to happen before the atom is knocked again — which is why strong storms show crimson crowns overhead.
- **Blue and violet/purple** come from nitrogen molecules at the bottom edge of the display, often visible as the lower fringe of a bright curtain.

Your eye matters too: faint green is far more visible to human night-vision than red, so cameras often show colours the naked eye misses — not a trick, just physiology.

## Reading the Forecast: the Kp Index

Space-weather services score expected geomagnetic activity on a 0-to-9 scale called **Kp**. Rough intuition:

- **Kp 0–2:** quiet; oval stays polar — the far-north latitudes (northern Scandinavia, northern Canada, Antarctica's coast) have the best odds.
- **Kp 3–4:** active; aurora reaches deeper into normally dark regions and mid-latitude sky-watching begins.
- **Kp 5–6:** storm; there are reports from continental mid-latitudes and occasionally from city skies.
- **Kp 7–9:** rare great storms; auroras have been recorded far into the tropics' neighbouring latitudes in history.

The Sun sets the long-range rhythm: activity rises and falls on an average eleven-year sunspot cycle, and the recent peak of the current cycle — Solar Cycle 25, which crested around 2024 — produced the strongest geomagnetic storms in two decades. Storms can arrive with only hours of warning from coronal mass ejections, so a "watch" released by a space-weather agency matters more than any seasonal calendar.

## A Practical Chase Plan

1. Pick dark dates: new Moon phase, clear climate, moonless nights, and get away from street lights.
2. Choose geography with history and infrastructure: northern Norway, Iceland, Finland's Lapland, Canada's Yukon and Northwest Territories, Scotland's north, and — for the southern version, the aurora australis — New Zealand's South Island and Tasmania.
3. Check the short-term forecast on the hour, not the week: a CME arrives within roughly one to three days of a big eruption, and warnings are issued as it approaches.
4. Once outside, face the **magnetic pole direction** (not exactly geographic north) and give your eyes twenty minutes to dark-adapt. A "faint grey glow brightening on its own" is a classic first report of an incoming aurora.
5. Photograph with a long exposure: 2–10 seconds at high ISO reveals structure the eye cannot.

## Aurora Beyond Earth

Jupiter and Saturn have enormous, permanent auroras driven by their intense magnetic fields and moons feeding them plasma; the Hubble and Juno missions have imaged them. An aurora is not an Earth exclusive — it is what any world gets when it has a magnetic field, an atmosphere, and a nearby Sun.
