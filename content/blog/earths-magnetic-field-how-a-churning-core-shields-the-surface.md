TITLE: Earth's Magnetic Field: How a Churning Core Shields the Surface
EXCERPT: A compass points north because the outer core is a molten iron dynamo in motion — and that same invisible field deflects the solar wind that would otherwise strip our atmosphere. Here is how it works, why magnetic north keeps wandering, and what a reversal would actually do.
TAGS: magnetic field, geodynamo, pole reversal, solar wind, magnetosphere, geomagnetism

## The Dynamo Under Your Feet

Earth's magnetic field looks like the one a bar magnet would make — field lines looping out of one hemisphere and back into the other — but a permanent magnet is impossible inside a planet: the core is far hotter than the Curie temperature at which iron loses fixed magnetisation. The real source is motion. The outer core is some two thousand kilometres of molten iron alloy, churning because the deep interior is hotter than the layer above it, and because the whole planet rotates, organising that churn into spiralling columns. Moving conductive fluid across an existing magnetic field generates electric current, and current reinforces the field that induced it: a self-sustaining **geodynamo**, converting the heat and rotation of the interior into a planetary-scale electromagnetic engine. The field it produces is not a perfect dipole: the magnetic north pole currently sits off the coast of the Canadian Arctic and the geomagnetic poles — where the field is vertical — are elsewhere again, and the whole pattern wobbles and drifts on timescales from days to centuries as the flow inside the core changes. We measure it constantly: observatories on the ground and satellites above track the field's strength — around twenty-five to sixty-five microteslas at the surface — and its slow secular variation, the same data that makes [migrating birds' internal compass](https://tukuk.org/blog/how-birds-migrate-the-compass-map-and-fuel-engineering-of-flight) worth studying.

## What the Field Actually Protects

The field's biological importance is indirect but enormous. The Sun constantly blows a stream of charged particles — the solar wind — past the Earth, and during strong events the pressure of that plasma would, without a magnetic shield, erode the upper atmosphere directly into space. The magnetosphere is the region where our field dominates: a compressed cavity on the dayside roughly ten Earth-radii out, stretched into a long magnetotail on the nightside, deflecting the charged particles around the planet the way a boulder deflects a river. When particles do leak in, they follow field lines into the polar regions and make the [auroras](https://tukuk.org/blog/what-causes-auroras-and-how-to-predict-one) — visible proof of the shield working, and the reason space-weather forecasts matter to satellites, power grids, and aviation. Mars is the cautionary comparison: it lost its global dynamo early, and the solar wind stripped most of what its atmosphere had been, leaving the cold, thin, radiation-swept surface the rovers now drive on. The technology dependence is newer than the science: a major solar storm hits unhardened electronics and long power lines, and the Carrington Event of 1859 — whose auroras were seen near the equator, and whose telegraph lines sparked and failed — is the benchmark a modern grid would experience as months of outage, according to realistic rather than sensational assessments.

## Wandering Poles and the Map Problem

Magnetic north is not the same point as the geographic north pole that maps are built around, and the angle between the two — declination — varies by location and by year, a fact every [map reader and navigator](https://tukuk.org/blog/how-gps-knows-where-you-are) eventually meets. Since systematic measurements began in the 1830s, magnetic north has travelled over a thousand kilometres northwestward, and since the mid-1990s it has accelerated to as much as fifty or sixty kilometres a year, crossing from the Canadian side of the Arctic toward Siberia — fast enough that the World Magnetic Model, the official navigation grid built on satellite and observatory data, had to be updated years ahead of schedule in 2019. The drift is core fluid in motion: jets of molten iron sloshing beneath the surface shear and reshape the field's fine structure. Meanwhile a broad region of weaker field — the South Atlantic Anomaly, where the inner radiation belt dips close enough to flip satellite electronics — sits under southern South America and the South Atlantic, a reminder that the field's surface pattern is lumpy and changing. And the field has flipped entirely before: the ocean-floor magnetic stripes that confirmed plate tectonics are the fossil record of reversals, basalt locking in whatever polarity the dynamo held as it cooled.

## What a Reversal Would — and Would Not — Do

Over the last few hundred million years the polarity has swapped dozens of times, on average every few hundred thousand years, but irregularly: the current normal polarity, the Brunhes chron, has run about 780,000 years, longer than average, which occasionally stirs pop-science fears of an imminent catastrophe. The geological answer is more nuanced. A reversal takes thousands of years — a few centuries of rapid change at most in the field's direction — and during the transition the field does not vanish but becomes weak and multi-polar, with several north and south poles scattered across the globe. The fossil record across past reversals, including the well-studied Laschamp excursion around 41,000 years ago, shows no linked mass extinctions: life survived, because the atmosphere itself absorbs the particle and ultraviolet radiation that a weaker field lets through — the magnetosphere protects the air, and the air protects us. The honest consequences of a reversal-era field are practical rather than apocalyptic: navigation systems and maps rewritten, higher radiation doses for satellites and polar aviation, more frequent aurora at strange latitudes. We are not reversing — the field's long-term weakening of about nine per cent over two centuries may be ordinary wobble, not the start of a flip, and dynamo models cannot predict the transition years in advance anyway. But the field is the quiet organ of the planet's habitability: generated two thousand kilometres down, felt by a compass needle, and standing — invisibly, constantly — between the surface and the wind from the Sun.
