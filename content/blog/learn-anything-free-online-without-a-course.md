TITLE: How to Learn Anything for Free Online — Without Enrolling in a Course
EXCERPT: You do not need a certificate or a subscription to learn physics, coding, or history. The hard part is not access, it is structure. Here is a repeatable method for self-teaching using free references, and how to keep from wandering.
TAGS: learning, education, self-study, science, reference, curiosity

## Access is solved; structure is not

Every fact below is available free, right now, on a phone with a browser. Textbooks have open equivalents, code has documentation, science has public references and encyclopedias. So the bottleneck for a self-teacher is no longer *finding* material — it is *ordering* it. Free content is a maze with no map, and the reason people quit is that they open nineteen tabs and learn nothing because nothing connected to anything. The fix is a small, deliberate structure you impose on yourself.

## The loop that actually works

1. **Pick one question you can answer today.** Not "learn astronomy" but "why do the seasons happen?". Narrow beats ambitious.
2. **Read one short, trustworthy answer first.** A concept page or encyclopedia entry gives you the skeleton before the details. Tukuk keeps plain-language concept explainers under its [science study hub](https://tukuk.org/study/science) and a browsable [encyclopedia](https://tukuk.org/wiki) for exactly this "get the shape first" step.
3. **Then chase the rabbit holes with search.** When the answer raises a new term, look it up deliberately, one at a time. Use a [search engine](https://tukuk.org/) that ranks its own index so you are following your curiosity, not someone's ad feed.
4. **Write three sentences from memory.** Retrieval, not re-reading, is what makes knowledge stick. If you cannot say it simply, you did not learn it yet.

That loop — narrow question, skeleton answer, targeted follow-ups, recall — is the whole method. It turns a pile of free links into an actual lesson.

## Make the sources agree

Free content is usually right on the big facts and occasionally wrong on the specifics. The cheap defence is triangulation: for any claim that matters, check two independent sources. For science and space questions, primary open data beats a blog's paraphrase — the [NASA data hub](https://tukuk.org/nasa) exposes live astronomy feeds so you can look at the same numbers the articles describe. When a reference and the raw data disagree, trust the data and stay curious about why.

## Curiosity is the syllabus

The advantage of self-teaching with no course is that you follow genuine interest, and interest is the strongest predictor of sticking with something. The trap is mistaking tab-opening for learning. Give every session a one-line goal, one skeleton read, and three sentences of recall, and a free evening becomes real progress.
