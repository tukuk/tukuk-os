## The Pyramid, When Teams Played Nine Forwards

Early football was essentially a rugby scrum with a kick — dribbling was the skill, passing was considered timid. The Victorian **2-3-5 "Pyramid"** was the first recognised structure: five forwards overwhelming full-backs, a half-back line shielding them. For decades the game was a numbers race, and the rules kept intervening: the offside law was rewritten in 1925 to require two defenders instead of three, instantly opening the game — and forcing everyone to redesign. Tactics history is partly rules history: change the constraint, and the shape changes.

## The WM, and the First Coach as Scientist

Herbert Chapman's **3-2-2-3 ("WM")** in the 1920s–30s answered the new offside by dropping a centre-half between the full-backs and creating wing-halves as a midfield engine. Arsenal won the league with it; the world copied it for twenty years. The lesson wasn't the shape itself — it was the idea that a *manager* could solve football with geometry, training drills and even floodlights. If you have ever wondered why the older numbering (1–11 shirts naming positions) still haunts modern squad lists, this is why: the numbers were written during the WM era, when a shirt literally reported your coordinate on the pitch.

## 1958, 1974: Two Shocks That Broke the Script

Brazil's **4-2-4** at the 1958 World Cup exposed the WM's slow middle to pace and interplay, and within a league season Europe had copied it. Then came **Total Football**: Ajax and the Netherlands in the early 1970s, where any outfield player could occupy any position — the fullback attacks, the striker defends, the space itself moves. The Dutch proved that structure beats personnel, and forced the world to answer with organisation rather than talent alone. Deep-lying playmakers, zonal marking and pressing triggers are all descendants of that argument; the tactical vocabulary you hear on commentary — "half-space," "third man run" — traces directly to those Dutch academies.

## Sacchi's Milan, Guardiola's Barcelona, Klopp's Liverpool

Arrigo Sacchi, a bank clerk turned coach with no top playing career, rebuilt Milan in 1987 as a pressing machine: an eleven-man unit compressing the pitch, not eleven individuals. **Zonal pressing** replaced man-marking the way factories replaced workshops. Twenty years later Guardiola's Barcelona made possession itself defensive — keep the ball, deny the counter, pass in triangles; the 4-3-3 with a false nine became the world's default for a decade. Jürgen Klopp's **Gegenpress** inverted it again: don't defend after losing the ball, hunt immediately *because* the opposition is disorganised at the moment of winning it — "the best playmaker is a counter-press." Each revolution was a response: pressing beat tiki-taka's slow build-up; transitions now beat both.

## Why Every Match Is Still This Argument

Watch any top game and you are watching tactics history live: a back three to pin the opponent's forwards (1930s), a false nine to confuse the centre-halves (2010s), a high line betting on the offside trap against through-balls (1980s), a low block daring crosses (Victorian). Football's genius is that nothing is ever dead — youth academies recycle old ideas against new personnel, and the rules keep moving: extra-time, substitutions, VAR, even the back-pass ban changed shape more than most managers admit. The same evolutionary logic runs through other sports on the world stage; cricket's T20 revolution compressed its tactics the way substitutions compressed football's, as covered in [how cricket works](https://tukuk.org/blog/how-cricket-works-a-beginners-guide), and the modern Olympic revival that made international fixtures a global calendar is told in [how the modern Olympics began](https://tukuk.org/blog/how-the-modern-olympics-began). Team histories, tournaments and standings for the world game live at [tukuk.org/football](https://tukuk.org/football) — and now, when a pundit says "they've gone to a back five," you know exactly which ghost that answer is invoking.
