TITLE: How Glaciers Carve the Land: U-Valleys, Moraines and Fjords
EXCERPT: A glacier is a river of ice — slow, heavy and relentless — and it reshapes mountains the way water does, just on a deeper timescale. Almost everything that makes alpine scenery look dramatic was sculpted by ice advancing and retreating.
TAGS: glaciers, geology, geography, erosion, science

## When Snow Outruns Melt

A glacier begins where, year after year, **more snow falls than melts**. Deep winter snow survives the summer, gets buried by the next snowfall, and over centuries is squeezed into dense, interlocked ice. That ice is a plastic solid: under its own enormous weight it deforms and flows downhill, and the whole mass creeps forward — typically only tens to hundreds of metres a year, slower than most people walk. A glacier moves by two mechanisms working together: the ice internally **deforms** like stiff putty, and, if there is meltwater at the base, the whole body can **slide** on a thin lubricating film. That motion, repeated over millennia, is what lets soft-looking ice cut solid rock. Glaciers store most of the world's fresh water, the frozen half of [the water cycle](https://tukuk.org/blog/how-the-water-cycle-works-evaporation-condensation-precipitation).

## Erosion: Plucking and Abrasion

Moving ice is an abrasive, bulldozing tool. It grinds away at the bedrock in two ways. **Abrasion** happens when rock fragments frozen into the base of the glacier scrape and sandpaper the floor beneath, leaving grooves and a flour-fine "rock flour" that gives glacial meltwater its milky colour. **Plucking** happens when meltwater seeps into cracks in the rock, freezes, expands, and rips loose blocks that the ice carries away. A glacier gouges downward far more aggressively than a river, because the whole mass — hundreds of metres thick and moving as a solid sheet — presses and drags across everything in its path.

## Landforms Carved by Ice

Where a river cuts a narrow, steep **V-shaped valley** (the story told in [how a river forms](https://tukuk.org/blog/how-a-river-forms-meanders-and-deltas)), a glacier widens and deepens that same valley into a flat-floored, steep-sided **U-shaped valley** — the signature trough of glaciated mountains. At the glacier's start zone, rotational slide-and-pluck carves a bowl-shaped **cirque**; where several cirques eat back into one mountain from different sides they leave a knife-edge **arête** or a pointed **horn** (the Matterhorn is the textbook example). Where a valley glacier's trough is later drowned by rising sea, you get a **fjord** — a deep, narrow inlet with near-vertical walls, like the coast of Norway. On the valley floor, ice streaming over an obstacle leaves a smoothed, asymmetrical bump called a **roche moutonnée**: gently ramped where it climbed, plucked-steep where it let go.

## What Ice Leaves Behind: Moraines and More

A glacier is also a delivery truck for debris, and its "load" marks where it reached. Rock it carries along the sides and centre is dumped as ridges of unsorted gravel and boulder called **lateral and medial moraines**; rubble pushed at the snout forms a **terminal moraine**, mapping the glacier's farthest advance. Under the ice, meltwater streams braid gravel beds, and when the ice finally wastes away it leaves **drumlins**, sinuous hills of till, and **eskers**, winding ridges that were once tunnels of subglacial rivers. Lone boulders stranded far from their parent rock — **glacial erratics** — are confetti from the retreat. Because glaciers erode upward and backward, the whole alpine landscape seen from a [high summit](https://tukuk.org/blog/how-tall-is-a-mountain-elevation-prominence-isolation) is essentially an ice sculpture mid-melt.

## Reading the Retreat

When climate warms, a glacier melts back faster than snow can rebuild it, and the ice front retreats uphill. That exposes freshly carved valleys, moraine dams that hold new meltwater lakes, and — importantly — tells us the ice once reached far lower, which is why ancient shorelines and moraine rings sit today above the surviving [glaciers](https://tukuk.org/glaciers) of the world. Ice is the slowest, longest-lasting sculptor in the mountains, and its chisel marks outlive it.
