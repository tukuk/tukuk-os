## "Tall" Is Not One Measurement

When someone asks which mountain is tallest, they usually mean one of three unrelated things, and mixing them up is the single most common error in peak-statistic debates. Geographers keep them separate:

- **Elevation** — height above sea level.
- **Topographic prominence** — how much a peak stands out from the surrounding terrain on its own.
- **Topographic isolation** — how far you must travel to reach any *higher* ground.

Each produces a different list of champions, and none is more "correct" than the others; they answer different questions.

## Elevation: The One Everyone Knows

Elevation measures from an agreed zero — mean sea level extended as a hypothetical surface (the geoid) — straight up to the summit. **Mount Everest**, at the 8,848.86 m value jointly surveyed by Nepal and China in 2020, is the highest point on Earth by this yardstick. Fourteen mountains cross the legendary 8,000 m "eight-thousander" line, all of them in the Himalaya and Karakoram, thrown up where the Indian plate drove into Asia.

But elevation above sea level is a slightly odd idea on a planet that is not a sphere. Sea level bulges at the equator, so the **farthest point from Earth's centre** is not Everest at all but **Chimborazo** in Ecuador, whose equatorial position more than cancels its lower elevation. "Highest" and "farthest from the core" are different questions with different answers.

## Prominence: How Big the Mountain Actually Feels

Prominence is the concept that best matches the visceral sense of a mountain's size, and almost nobody outside climbing circles knows the word. A peak's prominence is the minimum you must descend from its summit before you can climb up to any *higher* summit — in effect, how independent a peak it is. Everest's prominence equals its elevation, because nothing is higher and the "key col" is sea level.

**K2** is second in elevation but is a colossal, standalone titan; **Denali** in Alaska ranks only about 20th in elevation yet has the **third-greatest prominence on Earth** (after Everest and Aconcagua), which is exactly why it looms so massively out of the flat Alaskan interior. A bump on a high ridge may have a big elevation and a tiny prominence, because getting to a higher neighbour barely requires going down at all. Prominence is what separates real mountains from subsidiary summits, and it is why lists of "ultra-prominent peaks" — those with at least 1,500 m of prominence — number only in the thousands worldwide despite millions of named summits.

## Isolation: The Loneliness Score

Isolation measures horizontal, not vertical, distance: how far you must walk from a summit before encountering any higher point. By this metric the winner is again **Everest** (about 2,000 km to the next higher ground, essentially to the Hawaiian Islands), but the concept shines elsewhere — an island peak or an outlier in a flat plain can be dramatically isolated even when its elevation is modest. Isolation is the reason remote summit list-takers chase peaks that are unremarkable in height yet extraordinary in solitude.

## Relief: Base to Summit, One More Number

There is a fourth measure worth knowing — **local relief**, or vertical rise from base to top. By *that* standard the tallest mountain on Earth is **Mauna Kea** in Hawaii: only 4,207 m above sea level, but roughly 10,200 m from its base on the ocean floor, making it taller than Everest measured end to end. The distinction explains how a "short" hill sitting on the coastal shelf can out-rise a peak that pierces the jet stream.

Once you see that a single word — "tall" — hides elevation, prominence, isolation and relief, geography records stop being a list of numbers and become a set of questions you can answer precisely.
