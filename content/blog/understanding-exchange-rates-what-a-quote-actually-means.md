## Reading a Quote Out Loud

An exchange rate is always a ratio between two currencies, written as a pair: **USD/JPY = 150.4**. The first code is the **base currency** (the unit being priced), and the second is the **quote currency** (the money doing the pricing). So the number above means one US dollar buys 150.4 yen. Flip the pair and you get the reciprocal: one yen costs about 0.00665 dollars.

That ordering convention is fixed by market custom, not alphabet. USD, EUR, GBP, AUD and NZD traditionally sit on the base side; anything quoted against them uses their convention. This is why you will see EUR/USD but USD/CHF — and why copying "the rate" from one site into another that uses the opposite convention gives you its reciprocal. Same truth, inverted number.

## The Rate You See Is Not the Rate You Get

Public reference rates — like the ones the European Central Bank publishes daily and which drive most "live" converters — are **interbank mid rates**. They are the theoretical halfway point between what big banks will buy and sell to each other. You will never transact at the mid. What sits between you and the mid is the **spread**:

- **Bank and kiosk spreads** are wide. An airport counter might add 5–10% on top of the mid in the gap between its buying and selling price, plus sometimes a fixed fee. This is why €1,000 exchanged at an airport can be worth meaningfully less than the same €1,000 at a city-centre bureau.
- **Card networks** (Visa, Mastercard) use their own close-to-mid rates but the issuing bank may add a 1–3% foreign transaction fee on top.
- **Fintech transfers** compete mainly by shrinking the visible spread, which is why they can advertise "the real rate" — usually a 0.3–0.6% markup instead of 5%.

The practical rule: whatever number a converter shows you, treat it as the *anchor*, then ask how far the seller is from it. A "no fee" offer that is 2% worse on the rate is a 2% fee wearing a different hat.

## Why Rates Move — and Why the Direction Confuses People

Currencies are priced against each other constantly, so a "weak dollar" headline really means "a dollar that buys less of something else." Three forces do most of the short-term moving:

1. **Interest-rate expectations.** Capital flows toward the currency whose central bank is expected to offer a better real return. A rate hike — or even credible talk of one — tends to lift a currency before it happens.
2. **Growth and risk appetite.** In stressed weeks, money runs to traditional safe havens (historically USD, CHF, JPY); in calm weeks it reaches for higher-yielding, riskier currencies.
3. **Trade and capital flows.** Persistent importers of goods must keep buying foreign currency, which quietly pressures the domestic one over long horizons.

None of this makes rates *predictable*. Despite decades of attempts, no model reliably beats "tomorrow's rate is today's rate" over short horizons — a result known in academic economics as the Meese-Rogoff puzzle. Anyone selling you a sure forecast is selling something else.

## What This Means When You Convert

Before exchanging money, three checks save real amounts: compare the quoted rate against a mid-rate reference (the difference *is* the fee, even if labelled "free"), ask whether the shown total is before or after commission, and for cards, check whether the terminal is asking to charge in your home currency — that is dynamic currency conversion, usually a 3–7% trap, always decline and pay in the local currency.

The numbers are ECB reference rates — a faithful anchor, explicitly not a dealable quote.

## Sources and Freshness

The ECB publishes its reference rates around 14:15 CET each working day; weekends and holidays carry the last published Friday values, so a converter's "today's rate" on Sunday is genuinely Friday's. That is a feature of the data, not a bug in the page: reference rates are about accuracy, and accuracy has a timestamp.
