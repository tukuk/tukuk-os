TITLE: How Inflation Quietly Changes What Your Money Buys
EXCERPT: Inflation is the reason a banknote buys less every year even though the number on it never changes. It is not a mystery or a scam — it is a measurable, average rise in prices, and understanding how it is calculated and compounding makes your own money make more sense.
TAGS: inflation, money, economics, personal finance, prices

## What Inflation Actually Is

Inflation is a **sustained rise in the general price level**, which means the same amount of money buys a little less each year — a fall in money's **purchasing power**. It is different from one item getting pricier (a bad harvest raising food costs is not, by itself, "inflation"); inflation is when prices across the whole economy creep up together, and — crucially — it is measured as an average, so it hides the fact that some things (energy, rent) may race upward while others (electronics) actually fall. Deflation, the opposite, is prices generally *falling*, which sounds nice but can stall an economy as everyone delays spending.

## How It's Measured: The Basket

Governments track inflation with a **Consumer Price Index (CPI)**. Statisticians define a representative "basket" of what a typical household buys — food, fuel, rent, transport, clothing, healthcare and so on — with weights reflecting how much of a budget each consumes. Every month they price that basket, and the percentage change year-on-year is the headline inflation rate. Because the weights are averages, the official number will not match *your* personal inflation: if you commute far and spend on petrol, high fuel prices hit you harder than the basket suggests. This is the same distinction between an average and a specific case that shows up in [how exchange-rate quotes really work](https://tukuk.org/blog/understanding-exchange-rates-what-a-quote-actually-means).

## Where It Comes From

Economists describe a few overlapping causes. **Demand-pull** inflation happens when too much money chases too few goods — demand outruns supply and sellers raise prices. **Cost-push** inflation comes from the supply side: when inputs like oil, wages or imported goods get more expensive, producers pass the cost along. And there is a **monetary** view: when the money supply grows much faster than the economy's ability to produce things, each unit of currency is worth relatively less. In the real world these blend — a supply shock (cost-push) can trigger wage-and-price spirals (demand effects) if people expect inflation to continue.

## Mild Inflation Is the Target, Not an Accident

Most central banks deliberately aim for a low, steady inflation rate — commonly around **2% a year**. The reasoning is that slightly rising prices encourage spending and investment today (a reason a little debt becomes cheaper in real terms over time) while keeping a buffer against the greater danger of deflation. A predictable 2% is treated as the price of a smoothly running economy, and central banks steer toward it mainly by raising or lowering interest rates — expensive money slows spending and cools prices, cheap money does the reverse.

## Why a Small Rate Is Not a Small Effect

The catch with inflation is that, like interest, it **compounds**. A "just 3% a year" price rise does not take 100 years to matter — using the rule of 72, prices roughly **double in about 24 years** at 3%, and in 36 years at 2%. Someone saving cash under a mattress sees its real value eroded every single year, while a borrower with a fixed-rate loan quietly repays in money that buys less than when they borrowed it. That is the same compounding logic laid out in [how compound interest and loans work](https://tukuk.org/blog/compound-interest-and-loans-explained) — just pointed the other way, at what your money *keeps* rather than grows.

## What You Can Do With the Number

Knowing your real rate of return means subtracting inflation from the nominal one: a savings account paying 4% while prices rise 5% is losing you about 1% of buying power a year. Assets that tend to rise with prices (wages tied to inflation, some property, index-linked bonds) protect you; pure cash does not. None of this needs a forecast — just the habit of asking whether a return is measured in money or in what the money actually buys.
