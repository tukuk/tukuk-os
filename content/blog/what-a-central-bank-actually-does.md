TITLE: What a Central Bank Actually Does — Interest Rates, Money and the Last Resort
EXCERPT: The most consequential institution most people never chose is the one setting the price of money. Central banks sound like plumbing until your mortgage moves, then they sound like weather. Here is what they actually control, why independence matters, and where their power stops.
TAGS: money, central bank, inflation, interest rates, economics

## The Only Institution That Can Print Your Money

A central bank has three jobs that commercial banks cannot legally copy. First, **issuing currency**: the notes in your wallet are literally a liability of the central bank, which makes its credibility the foundation of the whole payments system. Second, **banking for banks**: every commercial bank holds settlement accounts at the central bank, so when one institution runs out of overnight cash at 3 AM, only the central bank can supply it — that is the "lender of last resort" role that calms panics, exercised in 2008 and again in March 2020. Third, and most visibly, **setting the policy interest rate**: the rate at which commercial banks borrow reserves. Every other interest rate in the economy — mortgages, business loans, deposits — is built on top of that base, which is why a single committee meeting can move a whole country's housing market. The mechanisms behind the numbers you see on the money hub at [tukuk.org/money](https://tukuk.org/money) all trace back to these three levers.

## The Thermostat: Rates Against Inflation

Modern central banking runs on one hard-won lesson: after two centuries of trying to micromanage economies, the institution that survives is the one that targets **price stability** — usually written as around 2% inflation a year. When inflation runs hot, the bank raises its rate: borrowing gets dearer, spending cools, prices slow. When the economy stalls, it cuts rates toward zero, and if that is still not enough, it does the unconventional things the 2010s made normal: buying government bonds directly ("quantitative easing") to push long-term rates down. The full chain — how a rate decision ends up changing what your groceries cost — is exactly what [how inflation quietly changes what your money buys](https://tukuk.org/blog/how-inflation-quietly-changes-what-your-money-buys) explains; the central bank is simply the actor that decision is about. And for anyone wondering why the local currency moves the way it does against others, the rate differential is the honest answer behind [what an exchange-rate quote actually means](https://tukuk.org/blog/understanding-exchange-rates-what-a-quote-actually-means).

## Why They Hide the Meetings Behind Closed Doors

Independence is the deliberate design choice that makes the thermostat possible. If an elected government could order rates cut before an election, borrowing would boom, inflation would follow, and the cost lands after the politicians have moved on — the same reason you do not let a driver adjust the speedometer to make the needle look better. Since the 1990s, most major economies give their central banks legal independence: appointees serve long terms, decisions are theirs alone, and accountability comes through published minutes and inflation reports rather than instructions. That design only works while the public tolerates it; the pressure returns whenever a rate rise makes mortgages dear, which is precisely the trade-off every society must keep re-deciding.

## Where the Power Stops

Central banks can set money's price but cannot set money's direction: a 2008-style crisis proved that cheap credit does not automatically flow to homeowners and factories, and quantitative easing did more for asset prices than for wages. They cannot fix supply shocks — a central bank had no answer for a pandemic shipping container or a closed gas pipeline, which is why 2021's "transitory" debate went wrong. And they cannot make an economy grow; that is technology, education and institutions. The honest summary of the most powerful financial institution on Earth: it manages the temperature, not the weather — and understanding what it can and cannot do is the difference between reading economics and believing it.
