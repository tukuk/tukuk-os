TITLE: How Scientists Know Where Animals Are — the Community-Science Answer
EXCERPT: Professional biologists number in the tens of thousands. The planet's species do not fit in that staffing plan. Here is how phone photos became a real data source, and what "research grade" means.
TAGS: wildlife, ecology, citizen science, inaturalist, biodiversity

## The Data Gap in Biology

Every conservation decision starts with a boring question: *where are they, and how many?* For the famous animals the answer is decent — collar studies, aerial counts, decades of museum records. For the overwhelming majority of species, the honest answer is "we do not know." Australia described more new species in the last decade than most people realise, and entomologists estimate most insects remain unnamed. You cannot protect a population you have never located.

The traditional instrument was the naturalist: a trained human walking around with a notebook. That instrument works, but it scales at the speed of one pair of boots. Something had to change in how observations get collected and, critically, *verified*.

## Photos Are Now Specimens

iNaturalist, launched in 2008 by two UC Berkeley students, replaced the notebook with the phone camera. The insight was not "people love taking photos of bugs" — that was obvious. The insight was adding a **social verification layer**: any user can suggest an identification, any user can vote, and the community's agreement level, not any single expert's word, decides what a record is worth.

Three quality tiers result, and they matter to anyone reading a species map:

- **Casual** — a photo, unverified or missing data. Useful for conversation, not for science.
- **Needs ID** — real observation data, but the community has not reached agreement. The pool where tomorrow's research-grade records sit.
- **Research grade** — enough independent agreement (typically community consensus on species level, with date, location and photo present) that the record is exported to GBIF, the Global Biodiversity Information Facility, the world's aggregator of occurrence data. These are the points you see on distribution maps in published papers.

There are now well over 100 million research-grade records. A 2020 analysis found the platform's weekly observations approached the combined output of every major museum in history — accumulated in twelve years rather than two centuries.

## Why the Skeptics Were Partly Right (and What Fixed It)

Early criticism was fair: crowds misidentify. Monarch and viceroy butterflies fool beginners; hundreds of moth species look like "a brown moth." The fix was not filtering out amateurs — it was the *second* amateur. Studies of the community-vetting pipeline found that agreement between independent non-experts tracks expert verdicts closely at family and genus level, and that the disagreement cases cluster exactly where taxonomy is genuinely hard. The platform's own bias-correction — expert curators arbitrating stuck records — then pushes the long tail into honesty: "genus-level research grade" is a real, useful, limited statement.

The genuinely unsolved problem is **sampling bias**: observers are concentrated near cities, near rich countries, and near charismatic animals. A map of "where the honey bee has been seen" is partly a map of where phone-owners walk. Ecologists now treat community data as a massive, biased, cheap prior — to be corrected with modelling, not thrown away. A million biased observations beat ten unbiased ones with an empty notebook.

## What the Data Does Once It Is Trusted

Research-grade occurrences power the IUCN Red List assessments that decide whether a species is "endangered" or "extinct in the wild." They drive invasive-species alerts — the first confirmed photograph of an Asian longhorned beetle in a new county triggers response teams. They feed climate-shift studies: European butterfly ranges have measurably crept northward by tens of kilometres per decade, and the evidence base for that headline is largely community photos. And they let a person in Kuala Lumpur contribute to a paper about a species they will never meet, which is the strangest, best part of the whole arrangement.
