TITLE: How Ocean Currents Work: The Conveyor Belt That Moves the World's Water
EXCERPT: The ocean is not a still pool with waves on top — it is a slow, planet-spanning circulation that steers weather, feeds fisheries, and moves more water than every river on Earth. Here is how wind, density, and the spin of the planet combine to run the conveyor.
TAGS: ocean currents, thermohaline circulation, gulf stream, upwelling, amoc, oceanography

## Wind Makes the Top Layer

Almost every surface current starts with the wind, and the first surprise is that water does not simply follow the air blowing over it. Because the Earth rotates, a moving parcel of water is deflected — rightward in the northern hemisphere, leftward in the southern — so the surface layer actually drifts at roughly a 45-degree angle to the wind. Each layer beneath is deflected a little further and moves a little slower, twisting into the **Ekman spiral**, and the net transport of the whole wind-touched top tens of metres is at ninety degrees to the wind itself. That sideways shove piles water up where winds converge and drains it where they diverge, and the piled-up water, trying to flow "downhill" under gravity, is what we experience as the great gyres: the clockwise spiral of the North Atlantic, the counterclockwise South Pacific, the Indian Ocean's seasonally flipping system that reverses with the [monsoon winds](https://tukuk.org/blog/the-monsoon-explained-how-a-season-of-winds-feeds-billions). Western boundary currents — the Gulf Stream, the Kuroshio, the Agulhas — are the narrow, fast return flows on the west side of each gyre, intensified because the Earth's rotation rate varies with latitude, a geometric effect that squeezes them into ribbons a hundred kilometres wide moving several knots.

## Density Makes Everything Deeper

At high latitudes the surface water does something currents aloft cannot: it sinks. Water gets denser when it cools, and in the freezing seas north of the Atlantic and around Antarctica it gets denser still when sea ice forms and salts are excluded from the crystal lattice, brining whatever liquid water remains. In the Nordic Seas and the Labrador Sea, winter cooling produces **North Atlantic Deep Water**, which plunges two to three kilometres down and spreads south along the bottom of the ocean basins. Near Antarctica the process goes further: the frigid, salty shelf water forms **Antarctic Bottom Water**, the densest natural water body on the planet, which fills the abyss of every ocean. This density-driven motion — thermohaline, from the Greek words for heat and salt — is the deep half of the global conveyor, and it is slow in the extreme: a molecule of deep water may take roughly a thousand years to complete the loop from sinking in the north to upwelling in the south Pacific and travelling back as warm surface flow. The conveyor moves, by some estimates, more water than all the world's rivers put together — just much, much more slowly.

## Where the Ocean Comes Up

The conveyor's most visible consequence happens where deep water is forced back into the light. Along the west coasts of continents in the trade-wind belts — Peru, northwest Africa, California, Namibia — persistent winds blow surface water offshore, and cold, nutrient-rich water rises to replace it in **coastal upwelling**. The nutrients are the remains of a century of sinking organic matter, and when light meets them the result is the most productive fishing ground on Earth: the Peru current alone has historically supported a fishery that fed a nation. When the winds weaken or the temperature pattern in the Pacific shifts — the El Niño phase of [ENSO](https://tukuk.org/blog/what-is-el-nino-and-la-nina) — the warm lid thickens, upwelling shuts off, and the anchovy collapse ripples from seabirds to markets within months. There is also slower, patchier upwelling in the open ocean, driven by currents meeting seamounts and by turbulence, and it matters on the global scale: the deep ocean holds most of the planet's dissolved carbon, and how much carbon returns to the atmosphere depends heavily on where and how fast that water surfaces.

## Why the Conveyor Is a Climate Argument

The conveyor's northward legs carry roughly a petawatt of heat into the North Atlantic — enough to explain why northwest Europe is far milder than Labrador at the same latitude, an honest framing that corrects the old exaggeration that the Gulf Stream alone gives Britain its climate (the prevailing southwesterly winds and the ocean's stored heat share the credit). Because the sinking engine is driven by cold and salt, adding warm freshwater to the subpolar North Atlantic — melting Greenland ice, stronger rain over the sea — makes the surface lighter and harder to sink, and the geological record shows the circulation has weakened and even collapsed in past climate upheavals, with temperature swings of several degrees in Europe playing out on decades-to-centuries timescales. Modern observation arrays in the Atlantic, together with fingerprint methods in temperature and salinity records, suggest the circulation has been somewhat weaker since the mid-twentieth century than before it — debated in magnitude, not in mechanism. The water moving beneath the weather is not a backdrop to the climate system; it is one of its main gears, and the [tides and waves you can see at the shore](https://tukuk.org/blog/why-the-sea-rises-and-falls-how-tides-work) are only the shallow skin of a machine that runs down to the abyss.

For anyone who has watched a river of weed cross the Sargasso or stood where the Agulhas rings peel off into the Atlantic, the conveyor is a reminder that the ocean behaves like a system, not a surface. Salinity maps, drifter buoys, and satellite altimetry now track its pulse the way weather stations track wind, and the picture they keep confirming is the same one the expeditions of the nineteenth century first sketched: a global, layered, slow-moving engine that decides where fish are caught, how heat is stored, and how quickly a warming atmosphere hands its energy down to the deep.
