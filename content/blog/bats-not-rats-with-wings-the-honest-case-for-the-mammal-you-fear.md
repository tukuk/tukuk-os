## A Fifth of All Mammals, and Almost All of Them Good

There are roughly 1,400 species of bat — about one in every five mammal species on Earth, the second largest order after rodents. Nearly all of them eat something humans would otherwise pay to remove: insects, fruit, nectar, pollen, vertebrates. Only three species, all in Central and South America, drink blood — the vampire bats, and even they mostly take a lick from sleeping birds and cattle, with an anticoagulant in their saliva (draculin) that medical science has since put to use treating stroke. The global image problem is out of proportion to the actual risk: a healthy bat population is infrastructure. In the United States alone, estimates put the pest-control value of bats at billions of dollars per year in avoided pesticide and crop loss, and the guano of insect-eating colonies fertilises the farmland beneath them for free.

The fruit-and-nectar side is even more decisive in the tropics, which is where the Malay-speaking world meets this most directly. The banana, mango, guava and durian of a tropical market all depend partly on animal pollination, and bats are among the most important of those animals — the ceiling-loom of a durian orchard, where a colony hangs by day and visits ten thousand flowers by night, is not coincidence; it is the reason the season has fruit at all. The wild case that matters most is the agave: the plants that make tequila are bat-pollinated, and industrial monoculture has made the partnership fragile enough that growers now maintain roosts deliberately. [How scientists classify life](https://tukuk.org/blog/how-scientists-classify-life-from-linnaeus-to-dna-trees) puts bats in their proper context — a rodent's cousin only at the very base of the family tree, and closer to a lemur than to either rat or squirrel in the details of their bones.

## Seeing With Sound: The Sonar That Beat Engineering

Echolocation was discovered in bats before the word existed: in 1794 the Italian scientist Lazzaro Spallanzani blinded birds and bats and watched only the bats survive their blindness. The mechanism — a laryngeal ultrasonic call, reflected off the world and read by ears so large and so mobile they function as steerable dishes — was proven with the 1938 Harvard experiments that flew blindfolded, ear-plugged, and nose-blocked bats past wires, and confirmed by Donald Griffin's naming of the faculty in 1944. The engineering is still ahead of us in parts: a vespertilionid bat emits dozens of calls per second in normal cruising, compressing to hundreds of "feeding buzzes" per second in the terminal attack phase, modulating frequency bands so finely that the Doppler shift of a fluttering moth's wingbeat shows up as a texture in the echo. Moths, in an arms race as old as the calls themselves, have evolved tympanic organs that hear bat ultrasound — and some tiger moths answer with jamming clicks of their own, an acoustic countermeasure.

The navigation question does not end at the ear. Noctule and fruit bats both use the magnetic compass like migratory birds, and nectar bats memorise flower locations with a cognitive map of orchards measured in square kilometres. The small-brained, feared creature is in fact one of the most sensor-rich animals in any ecosystem.

## White-Nose, Wind Turbines, and the Quiet Collapse

The honest case has a shadow, and it is not rabies panic — bat deaths from rabies exposure to humans are vanishingly rare — but the collapse nobody hears. White-nose syndrome, a cold-loving fungus (*Pseudogymnoascus destructans*) arriving from Europe in the mid-2000s, has killed something of the order of ten million bats in eastern North America, driving several hibernacula past ninety-nine per cent mortality, because a fungus thrives in the damp, stable cold where bats spend winter, forcing them to wake, burn fat reserves they cannot replace, and starve before spring. Wind turbines kill bats outright and by barotrauma at altitude, and the species most exposed are the migratory ones that fly high and late in the season — the exact behaviour that makes them valuable as pollinators.

Conservation for bats is mostly invisible, modest work: keeping caves and culverts closed to vandalism but open to airflow, putting bat boxes on the sheltered side of barns, leaving dead trees with splits standing in forests, and keeping porch lights off during migration — the same light pollution that scrambles [the star compass of migratory birds](https://tukuk.org/blog/how-birds-migrate-the-compass-map-and-fuel-engineering-of-flight) works on bats, whose insect prey gathers at it and drains the night's hunting yield. None of this is sentimental: it is pest control, pollination, and biodiversity maintenance done by an animal that costs nothing to feed, asks for a dark corner to roost, and returns the favour by eating the mosquito population of a county before dawn.
