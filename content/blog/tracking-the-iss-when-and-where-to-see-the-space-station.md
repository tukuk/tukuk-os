## Why the ISS Is Easy to See

The International Space Station orbits roughly 400 kilometres above Earth, circling the planet about every ninety-three minutes at nearly 28,000 km/h. That means it races across your sky in only three to six minutes, moving steadily from horizon to horizon with no blinking and no sound — the classic giveaway that you are looking at a satellite, not an aircraft.

You see it because sunlight reflects off its solar arrays. The station is invisible in Earth's shadow, so the best viewing windows are the hour or two after sunset and the hour or two before dawn, when the ground is dark but the ISS, 400 km up, still stands in sunlight.

## Reading the Live Position

Our live map at [tukuk.org/nasa/iss](https://tukuk.org/nasa/iss) plots the station's ground track over a day/night terminator computed from the actual Sun geometry, refreshed every fifteen seconds. Three things on that map tell you everything:

- **The ground track** (the red curve): the path of the point directly beneath the station. If the track will pass near your country in the next orbit, a visible pass is plausible.
- **The shaded night region**: passes that happen over the dark side are the ones you can catch from the ground, provided the station itself is sunlit at low elevation.
- **The sub-satellite point** (the labelled coordinates): where the station is directly overhead right now.

## What Makes a Pass "Good"

Astronomers rate passes by three factors:

1. **Maximum elevation** — how high the station climbs above your horizon. Over 40 degrees is excellent; under 20 degrees often means it sets behind buildings or haze.
2. **Brightness** — the brightest passes rival the planet Venus, because you see the full expanse of sunlit arrays and modules head-on.
3. **Sunlight fraction** — a pass that goes dark mid-flight has just entered Earth's shadow; the last few seconds before it winks out are photogenic and worth watching.

Because the orbit plane precess slowly relative to the Sun, visible-pass seasons at any given location last a few weeks, then fade for a few months. No pass for two weeks does not mean the ISS vanished — it means it is flying over you in daylight.

## A Simple Weekly Routine

Open the live map once in the early evening. Note the direction the track is moving at your longitude. The station repeats roughly the same sky path after a couple of days, so if you saw one pass, check again at the same time tomorrow and the next night. For formal pass predictions, any "pass calendar" tool computes them from the public orbital elements (the same two-line elements NASA releases), so cross-checking a listing against the live map is a good way to learn the sky.

## The Physics Behind the Sightings

The station's altitude decays continuously — atmospheric drag at 400 km is small but real — so reboosts are periodic, and the exact altitude shown on the live map drifts by a kilometre or so week to week. Its velocity is exactly what orbital mechanics demands at that height: about 7.7 km/s. That is fast enough to cross a continent before your eyes finish adjusting.

Watching the ISS is the single most accessible space activity on Earth: no telescope, no permit, no cost. Next clear evening, look up at the right minute and wave — a crew of up to seven astronauts and cosmonauts is aboard, covering each week enough distance to travel to the Moon and back several times over.
