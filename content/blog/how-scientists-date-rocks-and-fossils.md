TITLE: How Scientists Date Rocks and Fossils: Radiometric Dating Explained
EXCERPT: "How old is it?" is one of the deepest questions in science, and the answer comes from physics, not guesswork. Certain atoms decay like tiny, perfectly reliable clocks — and reading those clocks is how we know the Earth is 4.5 billion years old.
TAGS: radiometric dating, geology, fossils, science, physics

## Atoms That Tick at a Fixed Rate

Some atomic nuclei are unstable — **radioactive isotopes** — and they spontaneously transform into a different, stable "daughter" atom, releasing energy as they go. The remarkable fact that makes dating possible is that you cannot predict when one *individual* atom will decay, but in a large sample the *population* decays at a fixed, unchanging rate, measured as a **half-life**: the time for half of the parent atoms to turn into daughter atoms. Half-lives are unaffected by temperature, pressure or chemistry, so a radioactive isotope is a clock that keeps ticking no matter what happens to the rock around it. Different isotopes tick at wildly different speeds, from fractions of a second to billions of years, which is why scientists pick the isotope to match the age they expect.

## From Ratio to Age

Dating is a measurement of ratios. In a mineral that formed as a crystal, you can count how many parent atoms remain and how many daughter atoms have accumulated. Because each half-life halves the parent and adds a matching dose of daughter, the parent-to-daughter ratio tells you how many half-lives have passed since the crystal locked the atoms in. **Uranium-238 decays to lead-206** with a half-life of about 4.47 billion years — ideal for the oldest rocks and, indeed, for dating meteorites to set the age of the Solar System at ~4.54 billion years. **Potassium-40 to argon-40** (half-life 1.25 billion years) dates volcanic layers, and since [volcanoes](https://tukuk.org/blog/how-volcanoes-form-and-why-they-erupt) erupt lavas and ash that crystallise freshly, they give clean "zero the clock" moments.

## Carbon-14: The Clock for Things That Were Alive

For recent, once-living material there is **radiocarbon (carbon-14)**. Living things constantly exchange carbon with the atmosphere, so their carbon-14 matches the air. When they die, the exchange stops, and the carbon-14 already inside decays with a half-life of ~5,730 years. Measuring how much is left tells you how long ago the organism died — perfect for wood, bone, charcoal and cloth up to about 50,000 years old. Because the short half-life burns the signal out, carbon-14 cannot date dinosaurs or the oldest rocks; those need the slow uranium and potassium clocks instead.

## Dating Fossils Without Touching Them

Most fossils sit in **sedimentary rock** — layers of grains eroded from older rocks — and the grains themselves are older than the fossil, so you cannot date the sediment directly. Instead, geologists date the **igneous layers above and below** (a volcanic ash bed, a sill intruding through the sequence) and bracket the fossil's age between them. They also use **relative dating**: deeper layers are generally older (superposition), and certain short-lived species (**index fossils**) mark a known slice of time wherever they turn up. Combining the bracketing with radioisotopes is how the whole **geological time scale** got its numbers.

## Why the Clocks Can Be Trusted (and Checked)

A common objection — "what if the starting amount or the rate changed?" — is answered with cross-checks. Scientists use **multiple isotope systems on the same sample**; if uranium, potassium and other independent clocks agree, the date is robust. They also validate against **independently counted records** like tree rings (dendrochronology) and annual lake-bed layers, and they screen for signs that a rock was heated or disturbed, which can reset or smear a clock. Agreement between physics, stratigraphy and independent archives is what turns "the Earth is very old" from an assertion into a measurement — the same kind of scale that makes the [planet's extremes](https://tukuk.org/blog/geography-records-the-longest-largest-highest-on-earth) measurable in more than one way.
