TITLE: BMI Explained: What the Number Really Measures — and Where It Fails
EXCERPT: Body Mass Index is the most-used and most-misunderstood health number there is. Here is the arithmetic behind it, why the standard cut-offs exist, and the four situations where BMI flat-out misleads.
TAGS: health, bmi, body composition, fitness, nutrition

## The Two Numbers That Make the Third

Body Mass Index is deliberately crude, and that is its strength. You take your weight in kilograms and divide it by the square of your height in metres:

```
BMI = weight (kg) / height (m)²
```

For a person who weighs 70 kg and stands 1.75 m tall, that is 70 / (1.75 × 1.75) ≈ 22.9. The squaring of height is not decoration — it is an attempt to account for the fact that a taller body has more than proportionally more mass, so dividing by height alone over-penalises tall people. The result is a single index that correlates, *across a whole population*, reasonably well with how much body fat someone carries and with the health risks that come with it. That population-level correlation is the entire scientific basis of BMI — and the reason it is so often wrong for one specific human being standing in front of you.

## Where the Cut-Offs Come From

The familiar bands — under 18.5 "underweight", 18.5–24.9 "healthy weight", 25–29.9 "overweight", 30 and up "obese" — were drawn by looking at where the risk of disease starts to climb in large studies. The lower end tracks mortality and fertility risk rising as fat stores run too thin; the upper end tracks the risk of type 2 diabetes, cardiovascular disease and several other conditions rising as excess adipose tissue — especially fat carried around the abdomen — ramps up inflammation and insulin resistance. The boundaries are therefore about *averaged risk turning points*, not a cliff where a healthy body becomes an unhealthy one between 24.9 and 25.0. Two people at BMI 25.1 and 24.9 are, for practical purposes, not different; two people at BMI 25 can differ enormously in what actually matters.

## Four Ways BMI Misleads a Real Person

**Muscle versus fat.** Muscle is denser and heavier than fat for the same volume, so a heavily muscled athlete routinely lands in the "overweight" band while carrying very little fat and excellent metabolic health. BMI cannot tell a bodybuilder from someone with excess fat because it only sees total mass.

**Where the fat sits.** Two people at identical BMI can have opposite risk profiles depending on whether their fat is stored under the skin (comparatively benign) or around the organs in the abdomen (metabolically active and far more harmful). This is why a **waist measurement** and the **waist-to-height ratio** — aim for a waist under roughly half your height — add information BMI simply does not have.

**Age and sex.** Women naturally carry a higher share of body fat than men at the same BMI, and healthy older adults often sit slightly above the standard band with the lowest mortality, because a little reserve helps through illness. A single cut-off applied to an 18-year-old and an 85-year-old is a blunt instrument in both cases.

**Ethnicity.** The risk thresholds shift by population: at the *same* BMI, people of South and East Asian descent tend to develop metabolic disease at lower indices, which is why several countries define "overweight" and "obese" at lower cut-offs than the WHO global standard.

## So Is BMI Worth Bothering With?

Yes — as a cheap, instant, no-equipment **screening flag**, not as a diagnosis. Its job is to point out, at a population scale, who might benefit from a closer look. It works far better as a *trend* on your own body than as a single reading judged against a chart: watching whether your BMI is creeping up or down over months tells you something real about your trajectory, even if today's absolute number mislabels your body composition. The genuinely useful moves are to pair it with a tape measure and, when it matters, with direct measures like body-fat percentage, blood pressure and blood glucose — the things BMI is only trying to approximate.
