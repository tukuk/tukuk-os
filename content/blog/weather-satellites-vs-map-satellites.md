## Two Jobs in One Phrase

Ask for a "satellite view" and the internet offers two unrelated products wearing the same name, because two satellite families were built for two different questions.

**Geostationary weather satellites** — Himawari-9 over the Pacific, GOES-18 and -19 over the Americas, Meteosat over Africa and Europe — sit 35,786 km above one spot on the equator, orbiting at exactly Earth's rotation speed, so they hang motionless over a hemisphere. Their reward is *time*: one full-disk image every ten minutes, the reason typhoon-tracking pages can show a storm **spinning** and meteorologists can see a thunderstorm grow from puff to anvil between coffee breaks. Their price is *resolution*: from 36,000 km up, a pixel covers about half a kilometre of ocean, a few kilometres of land at the disc's edge. You cannot see your house. You can see your hurricane.

**Polar-orbiting mapping satellites** — Suomi NPP, NOAA-20, Sentinel family — instead fly north-south at roughly 800 km, crossing every point of the planet about once a day as Earth rotates beneath them. Their reward is *detail and coverage*: a pixel ~350–750 m wide for the whole globe, true-colour this time because they look straight down close enough. Their price is *now*: any single spot is seen only when the orbital stripe happens to fall over it, so the honest product is the **daily composite** — all of today's passes stitched into one complete picture, delivered hours later.

## Clouds Are the Real Divide

The two families also measure different light, and that decides what you can actually ask of them. Weather satellites carry **infrared and water-vapour channels** as their workhorses — they see cloud *tops* day and night, measure their temperature to infer altitude, and watch heat escape the planet; their pretty "visible" channel is just one of sixteen. Mapping satellites like VIIRS lead with **reflected sunlight across spectral bands**, tuned to tell vegetation from bare soil, smoke from cloud, sediment from clear water — plus that famous day/night band that reads city lights by moonshine. So when thick cloud covers a region (monsoon season, a cyclone's shadow), the weather satellite shrugs: "I see the cloud." The mapping satellites agree about the *top*, but the daily true-color pass at least shows you exactly how wide the cloud shield is and where the sunny break in it lives — for the ground beneath, nothing optical can help; that is radar's job, a third family entirely (Sentinel-1 sees through cloud, night and rain, by bouncing microwaves).

## Which Sky to Watch, Which Question

- **"Is the storm intensifying right now?"** → geostationary. The world weather map at [Tukuk Weather Maps](/weather/map) and official Himawari/GOES imagery show the same hemisphere refreshed every ten minutes — that is the only way to *watch* weather rather than read its report card.
- **"What did the Earth look like yesterday, everywhere?"** → polar orbiters. Smoke plumes from a wildfire, dust over the Sahara, green and brown coastlines: switch on the daily true-color layer on [Live Earth](/maps/sky) or [Maps & Navigation](/maps), or read [how free satellite imagery actually works](/blog/see-earth-from-space-today-free-satellite-imagery) for the machinery behind it.
- **"Where do people actually live after dark?"** → the day/night band, a mapping instrument, on the [night-lights layer](/maps/sky) — weather satellites' night IR channels see heat, not lamps.
- **"Under cloud, what changed on the ground?"** → radar satellites, the specialists in seeing through.

There is a quieter overlap worth knowing: both families share the same orbits' grandparent — the very first TIROS weather satellites of 1960 were also the first humans' drawings of Earth's whole cloud cover, and every "blue marble" image since descends from that surprise. The split into *film-the-hemisphere* and *map-the-surface* came later, from economics of altitude: park high and watch time, fly low and measure place.

## One Planet, Two Rhythms

Practically, the difference collapses into one habit: weather pages tick in **minutes over a part**, imagery pages tick in **hours over the whole**. Neither is "the live satellite feed" of movie fiction — the honest version is stranger and more useful: a planet watched on two clocks at once, ten minutes of motion overhead and one complete portrait every day, both arriving free, both one click away. Learn to ask each family only what it can answer, and the sky's entire report — today's storm, yesterday's surface, tonight's lights — fits in one browser tab.
