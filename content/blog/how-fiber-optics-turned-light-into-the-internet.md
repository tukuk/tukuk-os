TITLE: How Fiber Optics Turned Light Into the Internet
EXCERPT: Almost everything you have ever read online arrived as pulses of laser light bouncing down a strand of glass thinner than a hair. Here is the counterintuitive physics that makes that possible — total internal reflection, doped cores, and the amplifiers that let a signal cross oceans.
TAGS: fiber optics, internet, light, total internal reflection, bandwidth, networking

## The Idea: Making Glass a Conductor

A stream of water can carry a beam of light around a bend — the 1854 demonstration by John Tyndall with a leaking can is the famous classroom version — because when light inside a denser medium strikes the boundary with a less dense one at a shallow enough angle, it does not refract out at all; it reflects entirely back in. This is **total internal reflection**, and the critical angle that switches from "some light escapes" to "none escapes" is set by the two materials' refractive indices. An optical fibre is that trick made industrial: a core of ultra-pure glass with a slightly higher index, clad in glass with a slightly lower index, protected by polymer coating. Light entering the core within the acceptance angle ricochets down the length for kilometres, losing astonishingly little on each bounce — the modern miracle being not the reflection, which any pool of water does, but the *purity*: the first practical fibres in the 1960s lost such a fraction of light per metre that a signal could not cross a room, and the breakthrough was manufacturing glass clean enough that the loss fell below twenty decibels per kilometre, then below one. Today a photon can travel a hundred kilometres of fibre and still arrive detectable; the impurities that once scattered light are now counted in parts per billion.

## From Mirror Trick to Terabits

Sending "light on, light off" down a glass thread describes the first fibre links, but the reason a single strand now carries the traffic of a small country is that engineers stopped using one light and started using many. A fibre is multiplexed by wavelength: different-colour lasers (different frequencies) can be combined onto the same strand through **wavelength-division multiplexing**, each carrying its own independent data stream — sixty, eighty, a hundred and sixty colours at once, like every radio station in a city sharing one wire. Modulation advanced from on/off to schemes that encode bits in the phase and amplitude of the wave itself, squeezing many bits per symbol. The other enabling invention was the **erbium-doped fibre amplifier**: a short coil of special fibre, pumped by its own laser, that boosts the optical signal directly without converting it back to electricity — invented in 1987 and the reason transoceanic spans became routine, because regenerating every few kilometres electrically had been the hard ceiling on the capacity of undersea cables. One modern pair of fibres can push tens of terabits per second; the entire phone traffic of a mid-sized country fits comfortably in a strand you could tie around a pencil.

## Why Fibre Beats Copper on Every Axis That Matters

The practical case for fibre to the home is not just speed but physics-made-economics. Copper carries electrons and therefore suffers resistance (heat loss over distance), inductance and capacitance (signal smearing at high frequency), electromagnetic interference (the reason shielded cable exists at all), and ground-loop problems between buildings. Fibre carries photons and suffers essentially none of these: no electrical resistance to heat it, immune to radio noise and lightning, and safe to run beside high-voltage lines because glass conducts no current. Attenuation over a kilometre is a fraction of copper's, so a single laser reaches further; the cable itself is lighter than the copper it replaced, and the raw material — silica — is sand-adjacent in abundance compared to mined metal. The honest weaknesses are mechanical, not electrical: glass wants bending limits (a kink leaks light and eventually snaps), connectors need precision polish, and splicing takes a fusion machine that melts the two ends into one continuous strand. But none of that changes the verdict of the last thirty years: the global backbone is glass, the oceans are wired with it, and the last few hundred metres — the fibre-to-the-home decision — is simply the backbone reaching the stubbornly analog world of existing ducts and landlords.

The conceptual upgrade for anyone using the internet is realising what the abstraction layers hide: every request you make is a laser blink, every video call a hundred-and-sixtieth slice of one of those coloured beams, every round-trip delay you feel not processing time but the speed of light in glass — roughly two-thirds of c, which sets a hard floor of about five milliseconds each way across a continent that no amount of computing power removes. The [latency metrics that make a site feel fast](https://tukuk.org/blog/core-web-vitals-making-a-site-feel-fast) live on the wrong end of that floor: software can chase milliseconds, but physics owns the baseline. The internet is not a cloud. It is a very quiet, very clean set of lasers, whispering to each other through glass at the fastest speed the universe allows.
