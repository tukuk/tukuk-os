## The four-slot ceiling, and why lists smash into it

Ask someone to repeat back a string of digits they have just heard and the honest capacity is around four items — the old "magic number seven" was a generous average that newer research has trimmed. This is why an eight-planet list refuses to go in raw, why a fifteen-digit number is impossible as fifteen digits, and why the fix was never more repetition. The fix is *recoding*: one meaningless list becomes one meaningful string, and one long number becomes a handful of familiar groups.

Competitive memorists are not people with bigger memories; they are people who recode aggressively. And the three recodings they rely on most are ones any student can use on tonight's homework.

## Trick one: the acronym, and the sentence that carries it

Take the first letter of every item and read the resulting string as a word — the solar system's planets compress to `MVEMJSUN`, and medical students have run whole anatomy courses on similar abbreviations. When the letters will not form a pronounceable word, upgrade to a *first-letter sentence*: each item lends its initial to a line you make meaningful — the Great Lakes become "Super Huron Michigan Erie Ontario," a sentence that is also the list. The weakness of both is that letters are cues, not contents: they guarantee the order and nothing else, so pair them with the recall habit — say the sentence, then write the list from memory and check. A [memory kit](https://tukuk.org/alat/memory-kit) does the mechanical part: paste your list one item per line and it returns the acronym plus a generated first-letter sentence, ready for you to improve.

## Trick two: chunking, the reason phone numbers exist

Seven digits overload four slots, so the phone exchange broke them into 3-4 groups and made them rememberable — the same trick works on any long string: bank references, constant digits, a spine of dates. Fifteen digits become `0198 3746 5512 038`, four groups you can actually hold. The same principle organises bigger material: a history of eight causes is not eight items, it is two groups of four; a syllabus is four subjects, not forty topics. Chunking is less a memorising trick than a *scheduling* trick — it decides what one recall attempt is about. The [memory kit](https://tukuk.org/alat/memory-kit) chunks any number you paste, and the drill worth building on top of it is mental arithmetic: a [times tables trainer](https://tukuk.org/alat/times-tables) turns `7 × 8` from a calculation into a retrieved fact, which is chunking applied to numbers themselves.

## Trick three: the ridiculous image, the one no generator can do for you

Mnemonic research is quietly consistent about one thing: *distinctiveness survives*. The acronym everyone in the class already knows is noise; the sentence with your grandmother skydiving in it is not. Double-word spelling errors die the same way — `necessary` keeps its single *c* and double *s* best in an image silly enough to be memorable, which is why [spelling practice](https://tukuk.org/alat/spelling) hides words one letter at a time instead of listing them, and why fact sets stick when they are games: the [flags quiz](https://tukuk.org/alat/flags-quiz) and [word scramble](https://tukuk.org/alat/word-scramble) attach the retrieval to a small drama of four options and a streak.

The final step of every generated acronym is the one you own: swap the boring word for the absurd one. Images that embarrass you are the ones that stay.

## What to memorise this way, and what not to

Recoding is for *arbitrary* material — the lists, orders and spellings that have no logic doing the binding for you. Anything with structure is learned better by finding the structure: the periodic table's groups rhyme chemically, an essay's points bind logically, a language's grammar is a system, not a string. The honest division of labour: understand what can be understood, recode what cannot, and test both closed-book — because, as [the active-recall research](https://tukuk.org/blog/active-recall-why-testing-yourself-beats-re-reading) keeps showing, the trick earns its keep only when you practise pulling it out.
