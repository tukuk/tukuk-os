TITLE: Why the Sky Is Blue and Sunsets Are Red: One Effect Explains Both
EXCERPT: Sunlight looks white but is every colour at once. The reason the daytime sky is blue and the evening one is orange is the same scattering trick — and it also explains why space is black and clouds are white.
TAGS: sky, science, light, weather, atmosphere

## White Sunlight Is a Rainbow in Hiding

Sunlight looks colourless, but it is a blend of every visible wavelength, from short **violet and blue** waves to long **orange and red** ones. A prism, or a raindrop, can fan that blend back out into a rainbow. The colour of the sky is not the Sun's own colour at all — it is what happens to that white light when it smacks into the air above your head.

## Rayleigh Scattering: Blue Gets Bounced Around

Earth's atmosphere is mostly nitrogen and oxygen molecules, and they are far smaller than the wavelength of visible light. When light hits particles that tiny, it scatters — and the amount of scattering depends sharply on wavelength. This is **Rayleigh scattering**, and it goes roughly as **1/λ⁴**: shorter wavelengths scatter enormously more than longer ones. Blue light, being short, is thrown in every direction by the molecules; red light, being long, mostly sails straight through.

So when you look at any part of the sky away from the Sun, you are seeing sunlight that has been scattered sideways — and because blue is the colour that scatters best, that sideways light is blue. The whole dome becomes a soft glow of redirected blue. The technical wrinkle is that violet scatters even more than blue, yet the sky is not violet: the Sun emits less violet to begin with, our eyes are less sensitive to violet, and the upper atmosphere absorbs some of it — so the mix lands as blue.

## Why Sunsets Turn Red

The same rule, applied to a longer journey, gives you the orange hour. Near sunset the sunlight must travel through a much **thicker slab of atmosphere** to reach your eyes at a low angle — several times more air than at midday. On that long path, almost all the blue gets scattered out of the beam before it arrives, leaving the longer-wavelength reds and oranges to punch through. The Sun itself looks reddened because you are seeing what survived the long trip: the colours that scatter least are the ones that make it to you.

Sunsets are more dramatic still when there is dust, smoke or pollution aloft, because larger particles enhance the effect and paint the scattered light into deeper reds — which is why volcanic haze and wildfire smoke are famous for spectacular evenings.

## The Same Physics Explains Clouds and Space

Once you see the mechanism, a cluster of ordinary facts snaps into place at once.

- **Space is black** because there is no atmosphere to scatter light sideways. With nothing to bounce the Sun's rays around, the sky stays dark even in daylight, and the Sun is a blinding disc in a black field — as every astronaut photograph shows.
- **Clouds are white** because they are full of water droplets far larger than light's wavelength. Big droplets scatter all colours roughly equally (this is not Rayleigh scattering), so cloud light is scattered white. A thick cloud looks grey simply because so much light is absorbed or reflected aloft that less reaches the bottom.
- **The sky near the horizon is paler** because that line of sight passes through more air with more larger particles, mixing in scattered whites and washing out the deep blue of the zenith.

None of these are separate rules. They are one scattering effect read at different angles, over different distances, and through different particles — which is the most satisfying kind of explanation in science.

## Where the Sky Gets Weird

Understanding scattering also lets you appreciate the exceptions. On planets with different atmospheres the sky is a different colour: a haze of hydrocarbons makes **Titan's sky orange**, and a thin, dusty, scattering-poor **Martian sky** is butterscotch by day and, famously, glows blue around a setting Sun — the opposite of Earth, because Mars's fine dust scatters blue light forward near the Sun's disc. The colour of a sky is really a fingerprint of what the air above it is made of.
