## Three Words, One Object, Three Situations

The vocabulary trips people up constantly, but it is precise. A **meteoroid** is the solid particle in flight through space — anything from a grain of sand to a boulder-sized rock. A **meteor** is the *light phenomenon*: the streak of ionised gas produced when that particle slams into the atmosphere at between eleven and seventy-two kilometres per second and vaporises in a column of plasma hotter than the sun's surface. And a **meteorite** is what is left if any part of the original particle survives to hit the ground. The shooting star is not the rock at all — the rock is gone, atomised forty kilometres up — the shooting star is the scar it wrote in the air.

## Why Every Shooting Star Is Old Comet Dust

Most meteors are not asteroids falling; they are debris streams. A comet shedding dust along its orbit leaves behind a torus of particles roughly the width of its tail, spread around the full ellipse over centuries. Earth's orbit intersects several of these streams, and every year at the same dates — because Earth reaches the same point in space at the same time each year — we plough through them and get a **meteor shower**: the January Quadrantids, the May Eta Aquariids (Halley's inbound dust), the August **Perseids** (from comet Swift–Tuttle), the December **Geminids** (from the rocky object 3200 Phaethon, likely a dead comet). In a shower, all meteors are travelling parallel to each other through space, but appear to radiate from one point in the sky — perspective, exactly like parallel railway lines meeting at the horizon. That radiant gives each shower its name: Perseids appear to come from the direction of Perseus, one of the [seasonal anchor constellations](https://tukuk.org/constellations) every stargazer learns.

Rates peak at fifteen to a hundred per hour under dark skies, and viewing is one of astronomy's cheapest pleasures: no telescope, no alignment, no light pollution rules beyond finding any darkness. Lie on a mat, face the half-sky away from the moon and the brightest constellations, give your eyes thirty minutes to adapt, and watch for two hours before dawn — the local sky, rotating into the direction Earth's orbit is travelling, faces the oncoming debris after midnight the way a windshield collects more rain than the rear window. Peak dates drift only a day or two per year, so any calendar of the sky is good for several seasons; [moon phase timing](https://tukuk.org/blog/moon-phases-explained-what-you-are-actually-seeing) matters more, because a half-lit moon overhead can halve your visible count.

## What Survives — and What the Survivors Teach

Fireballs — meteors brighter than Venus — come from fist-sized or larger bodies, and only a small fraction of them drop meteorites that reach the ground, because much of the mass ablates and the remainder slows to terminal velocity and simply falls. The pieces we find fall into three families, and their composition is a direct read-out of how the solar system assembled itself. **Iron meteorites** are the sliced-open cores of differentiated planetesimals — worlds that melted early, separated metal from rock, and were later shattered by collisions; cutting and acid-etching one reveals the Widmanstätten pattern, interlocking metal crystals that could only have grown by cooling a few degrees per *million* years, inside a body tens of kilometres across. **Stony-irons** are core–mantle boundary material. **Stones** are crust and mantle rock — and within them, the carbonaceous chondrites are the most precious objects in any laboratory: unchanged dust from the solar system's birth, containing amino acids and water-altered minerals, evidence that the ingredients for life were manufactured before Earth even finished forming.

The practical lessons are older and stranger than the science. Because meteorites often land in fields where finding a dark rock against pale soil is possible, recovered falls have repeatedly turned up iron where no iron geology exists — which is why, for most of human history, metal that would not rust and came "from the sky" was genuinely valued as meteoric: some of the earliest smelted iron artefacts, from Anatolia to Egypt (Tutankhamun's dagger is one), are now confirmed to be meteoritic, predating the iron age proper. And in the modern era the sky still delivers: the 2013 Chelyabinsk airburst — a twenty-metre rock that detonated with the force of several hundred Hiroshima bombs, injuring fifteen hundred people with shattered glass rather than impact — was a reminder that the debris stream Earth swims through is not merely pretty, and that the same orbital neighbourhood that gives us the August Perseids occasionally delivers the bill.
