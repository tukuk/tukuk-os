TITLE: Undersea Cables: The Physical Internet Under Your Ocean
EXCERPT: Ninety-seven per cent of intercontinental data — every email, transaction and stream crossing an ocean — rides on cables you can walk past on a beach. Here is where they land, how they survive the seabed, and what happens when one breaks.

## The Map Nobody Sees

The mental model of the internet as a "cloud" collapses the first time you look at the actual topology. Radio and satellites handle the last mile and the edge cases; the overwhelming majority of data crossing oceans — an estimated 97 per cent or more of intercontinental traffic — travels through **submarine fibre-optic cables** laid on the seabed, roughly 500,000 kilometres of them across more than four hundred active systems, with new ones added every year to chase demand. Their routes are old geography, not digital logic: they cluster where shipping and empire have always funnelled traffic — through the Red Sea and Suez, the Strait of Malacca, the Bay of Biscay, the Suez-to-Marseille and Mumbai-to-Marseille corridors, the trans-Pacific landings at Hawaii-side repeater points. A handful of cable-dense chokepoints carry a disproportionate share of the world's connectivity, which makes their geography a strategic fact the way the Panama Canal is a strategic fact: a single dredger anchored in the wrong shipping lane once severed a dozen cables at the Suez landing and degraded internet across half of Asia.

## Laying and Surviving a Cable

A transoceanic cable is not one rope but an engineered stack, different by depth. In shallow water near the landing — where anchors and trawls live — the fibres ride inside steel armature wire and a copper or aluminium sheath that also carries the power, buried a metre or more under the seabed by a plough. In the deep abyssal plain, where nothing scrapes except rare currents, the cable thins to a smooth, waterproofed pencil that can lie on the sediment for decades. The fibres inside are the same total internal reflection as any land fibre; what makes an ocean span possible is the **repeater**: every sixty to eighty kilometres, a sealed amplifier module — the same erbium-doped optical design as on land, but built to run for twenty-five years at a thousand volts of continuous DC power from the cable itself and to be untouchable at eleven thousand metres of pressure. Landing stations, at the beach end, convert light back into routing and hand traffic to the land networks — an unmarked building in a quiet coastal town that is, functionally, a port of entry for the entire internet of a country.

The maintenance model is industrial-scale patience. Ships carry cable in tanks the size of railway carriages; a fault is located by optical time-domain reflectometry — measuring the round-trip time of light pulses to pinpoint the break within hundreds of metres — then a robotic grapnel finds the cable on the seabed, brings a loop to the surface, cuts the damaged section, and splices two new tails together mid-ocean with joints made in a clean-room cabin aboard. Repairs take days to weeks depending on depth and weather. Every fibre is tested before shipping because a splice error at 4,000 metres is a round trip, not a fix.

## Fragility, Sovereignty, and the Shark Myth

Physical cuts are blamed for outages far more often than the famous 2007 memo claimed — the spate of "shark-chewed cables" reports turned out mostly to be historical, and today's leading causes are fishing anchors, trawling, ships dropping gear, and storms exposing shallow sections. The fragility is administrative as much as physical: because cable landing rights, repair fleet capacity, and monitoring are national assets, an outage in one jurisdiction can strand traffic for a region, and the geopolitics of who builds and owns each new system is now open diplomatic argument. Cable protection regimes — treating interference with civilian communications infrastructure as a matter of international law — date from the 1884 Paris Convention for the Protection of Submarine Telegraph Cables, a treaty older than aviation, and the principles have changed surprisingly little: intent to damage a cable is a crime in most coastal states, and repair ships get passage courtesies the way hospital ships once did.

The conceptual takeaway for anyone who has ever cursed a slow intercontinental video call is that latency across an ocean is not mysterious; it is a physical route with a length. Light in fibre covers about 200,000 kilometres per second, so the London-to-New York minimum round trip is about sixty milliseconds even in perfect conditions, and the actual cable paths — which bend to avoid deep trenches, historic shipwreck zones, and the territorial waters of countries that would rather the data did not land there — are longer than the great-circle distance. The [maths of speed-of-light floors](https://tukuk.org/blog/how-fiber-optics-turned-light-into-the-internet) is why the fastest networks optimise *which cable* your packet rides, not how hard it tries. The ocean, which looked like the obstacle between continents since the first sail crossed one, has become the most engineered surface on Earth — wired floor to skin, quieter than any highway, carrying the whole of the human conversation a few hundred metres beneath a fishing boat.
