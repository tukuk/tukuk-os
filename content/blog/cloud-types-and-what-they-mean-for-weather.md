## Clouds Are a Height and Shape Code

Meteorologists classify clouds along two axes at once: **how high they sit** and **what shape they make**. Learn the small Latin vocabulary and the sky becomes legible.

The height prefixes are:
- **Cirro-** — high clouds (above roughly 6,000 m), made of ice crystals because it is that cold up there.
- **Alto-** — mid-level clouds (roughly 2,000–6,000 m).
- **(no prefix)** — low clouds and vertical developments.

The shape words are:
- **Cumulus** ("heap") — puffy, lumpy, cauliflower clouds with flat bases.
- **Stratus** ("layer") — flat, sheet-like layers that smear across the sky.
- **Cirrus** ("curl") — thin, wispy, hair-like streaks.

Cross the two and you get the ten basic types. **Cumulonimbus** — the tower of an approaching thunderstorm — is the most important one to recognise, and it belongs to no single height because it builds from low to very high all at once.

## The High Clouds: A Warning of Change

Thin, feathery **cirrus** clouds are almost pure ice crystal, brushed into streaks by the jet stream high above. They look harmless, and alone they are — but cirrus thickening and spreading over hours is one of the classic signs that a **warm front is approaching**. The sequence is reliable: high wisps first (the far leading edge of a front), then **cirrostratus** (a milky veil that makes a halo around the Sun or Moon), then lower and thicker cloud, then rain. Watch cirrus thicken across the sky and you are usually watching a frontal system crawl in from the west, often with 12–24 hours of warning.

## The Puffy Clouds: Fair, or Fierce

**Cumulus humilis** — small, sharp-edged cotton-wool heaps with flat bottoms, appearing on a sunny morning and vanishing by evening — are the fair-weather clouds of a convective day. The Sun warms the ground, the ground warms a parcel of air, and that air rises, expands and cools until its water condenses into a cloud base. The flat bottom marks the exact altitude where condensation starts.

But on a hot, moist day those same heaps keep growing *upward* into **cumulus congestus** towering cauliflower stacks, and eventually into the anvil-topped **cumulonimbus**. A fair-weather cumulus at noon that has become a dark, over-reaching tower by late afternoon is the visible anatomy of a thunderstorm being born. This is why summer storms often build from clear skies in the afternoon: the engine is the day's own heating.

## The Layer Clouds: Damp and Grey

**Stratus** is a featureless grey blanket, essentially fog that is not touching the ground. It gives a dull, drizzly day with low visibility but rarely dramatic weather. **Nimbostratus** — the "rain layer" — is thicker, darker stratus that delivers steady, long-lasting, widespread rain or snow; a day ruled by nimbostratus is grey and wet for hours on end, not a passing shower. When you see a low uniform ceiling with continuous precipitation, that is nimbostratus working.

## Reading the Next Few Hours

Put the families together as a forecast tool. A sky of scattered puffy cumulus with blue between means rising air is local and shallow — fair. Cirrus and haloed Sun means a front is miles away but coming. A descending staircase of cloud types thickening overhead means the front is close and the rain is not far. A single dark towering cumulonimbus with a smooth anvil top means hail, lightning and a gust line under it, and you want to be inside. Clouds are the atmosphere's own handwriting; you just have to learn the alphabet.
