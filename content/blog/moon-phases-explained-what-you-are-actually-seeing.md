## One Rock, One Light, Three Players

Every moon phase is the same simple geometry: the Sun lights half of the Moon at all times, and as the Moon orbits Earth (one lap roughly every 27.3 days against the stars), we see that lit half from different angles. The cycle of shapes you notice from your backyard — new to full and back — takes **29.5 days**, slightly longer than the orbit itself, because Earth is also moving around the Sun and the Moon has to "catch up" to the same Sun-Earth-Moon angle. Astronomers call that 29.5-day shape cycle the synodic month, and every calendar that counts months owes the word to it.

## The Eight Shapes in Order

1. **New Moon** — the lit side faces away from us; the Moon sits near the Sun in the daytime sky and sets soon after sunset.
2. **Waxing crescent** — a thin right-hand sliver (for mid-northern viewers) low in the west just after sunset. "Waxing" means growing.
3. **First quarter** — half lit, right side. It is called "quarter" because the Moon is a quarter of the way around its phase cycle, even though it looks half — one of the language traps of astronomy.
4. **Waxing gibbous** — more than half, still growing; rises later in the evening with each night.
5. **Full Moon** — Earth is between Sun and Moon, so the entire near face is lit; it rises at sunset and sets at sunrise, ruling the night.
6. **Waning gibbous** — shrinking now, "waning," and rising noticeably later each night.
7. **Third (last) quarter** — left half lit; best seen in the morning sky.
8. **Waning crescent** — a thin left-hand sliver low in the east before dawn — the pre-sunrise Moon that poets loved.

If you live in the southern hemisphere, the growth direction mirrors: the waxing Moon fills from the left. The phase names and timing are identical everywhere; only the mirror image flips.

## The Tricks the Moon Plays

**Earthshine.** On a thin-crescent evening you can often see the dark part of the Moon faintly glowing — sunlight reflected off Earth's oceans and clouds onto the lunar night, then back to you. Leonardo da Vinci explained it in the 1500s; the old poetic name was "the old Moon in the new Moon's arms."

**Why we always see the same face.** The Moon is tidally locked: Earth's gravity raised a bulge in the Moon long ago, and friction slowed its spin until one rotation exactly equals one orbit. Far from being airless and dead, the Moon's geography — the dark maria that form the "man in the Moon" — is ancient basalt from volcanic floods, and we never see its far side from the ground; the first human eyes on it belonged to Apollo 8's crew in 1968.

**Why eclipses do not happen every month.** The Moon's orbit is tilted about 5 degrees to Earth's orbit around the Sun, so on most full moons the shadow misses — lunar eclipses and solar eclipses need the phase and the crossing point to align. A total solar eclipse also requires near-perfect coincidence of size and distance: the Sun's diameter is about 400 times the Moon's, and it is also about 400 times farther away, so the two discs cover each other almost exactly in our sky.

## Use It as a Clock

Phases are a free, universal calendar. Sunset crescent = early in the lunar cycle; midnight full Moon = middle; predawn crescent = late. Tidal ranges spring and neap with the phase geometry (sun-and-moon aligned at new and full = the biggest tides). Sailors, fishermen and night hikers have read the phase as a clock for millennia — and you can start tonight by simply noting which side is lit and asking the geometry why.
