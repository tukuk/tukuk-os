## The styles are arguments about what makes a source findable

A reference is a promise that someone else can find the exact words you used. Every citation style is one culture's answer to *which details matter most* — and once you see the answer each style is giving, the punctuation stops being arbitrary and starts being memorable.

**APA** (American Psychological Association, 7th edition) is the science style, and it hides nothing about age: the year sits right after the author, in parentheses — `Zimmerman, B. J., & Schunk, D. H. (2011). Handbook of self-regulation... Routledge.` In a field where a 2011 finding may be obsolete by 2026, the reader checks the date before the title. Sentences title-case the article, italics the book, and an in-text call is tiny: (Author, Year).

**MLA** (Modern Language Association, 9th edition) is the humanities style, and it buries the year at the end — `Zimmerman, B. J., and D. H. Schunk. Handbook of Self-Regulation... Routledge, 2011.` A nineteenth-century novel is not out of date, so the date is the least urgent fact; what matters is the *location within* the source, which is why in-text calls are (Author 47) — a page number, because you and your reader are meant to be able to open the book at the exact line. Note also the spelled-out "and" and the title-case titles: MLA treats the title itself as a work of art worth preserving.

**Chicago** actually has two systems. *Notes and bibliography* serves history — footnotes carry the argument and the bibliography at the back, with the publication data in a relaxed order. *Author-date* is APA with different commas, used by the social sciences that prefer Chicago house rules. If the assignment says Chicago, ask which one; it is a real question.

**Harvard** is not one organisation's style but a family of author-date conventions standard across UK and Commonwealth universities: `Zimmerman, B.J. and Schunk, D.H. (2011) Handbook of self-regulation... Routledge, London.` No full stops between initials, no ampersand — small differences that markers notice precisely.

## Where the pain actually lives

Not in the theory. In the punctuation: who gets a full stop after the initials, whether the publisher or the website comes first for a webpage, and the one genuinely modern question — **access dates**. A book citation promises a stable object; a webpage citation promises a moving target, which is why the newer styles grew the "Accessed 5 November 2026" clause and why you should screenshot anything you cite with no author, no date and no sign of maintenance. Webpage entries differ most between styles: APA keeps the site name and the bare URL, MLA quotes the page title and italicises the site, Harvard writes "Available at: … (Accessed: …)".

The one true error is not a misplaced comma — it is mixing styles inside one reference list.
