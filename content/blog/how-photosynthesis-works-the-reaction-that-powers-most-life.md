TITLE: How Photosynthesis Works: The Reaction That Powers Most Life
EXCERPT: A leaf is a solar factory that assembles sugar out of air and water, and the oxygen you are breathing is its waste product. Here is the two-act machinery of photosynthesis — the light reactions and the Calvin cycle — and why the details explain C4 crops, houseplants, and the entire food chain.
TAGS: photosynthesis, calvin cycle, rubisco, chlorophyll, thylakoid, biology

## The Deal at the Centre of the Biosphere

The summary equation — carbon dioxide plus water, driven by light, yielding sugar plus oxygen — is taught to children and hides an engineering marvel. Photosynthesis is the process by which energy carried by photons is converted into chemical energy stored in carbon-carbon and carbon-hydrogen bonds, and almost every food web on land, and most ones in the sea, are branches of that one reaction: the calories in your breakfast are, within a year or two of their origin, sunlight fixed by a plant, and the [oxygen that ocean and forest put into the air](https://tukuk.org/blog/phytoplankton-the-invisible-forest-that-feeds-the-ocean) came from splitting water. The chemistry is hard because carbon dioxide is a stubbornly idle molecule; sticking energy into it requires a series of precisely ordered steps performed in the chloroplast, the organelle that evolved when an ancestral plant cell swallowed a photosynthetic bacterium and kept it working. Life found other routes — chemosynthetic bacteria at [deep-sea vents](https://tukuk.org/blog/life-in-the-deep-sea-what-survives-under-crushing-pressure) build sugar from hydrogen sulphide instead of light — but they are the exceptions that prove the rule: the biosphere runs on photon catching.

## Act One: Tearing Water Apart with Light

The **light reactions** happen in the thylakoid membranes, stacked pancakes of green tissue inside the chloroplast. Chlorophyll — the pigment that makes leaves green because it reflects the green band of sunlight while absorbing red and blue — is the antenna, but the machine is bigger than one molecule: protein complexes arrange hundreds of pigment molecules so that an absorbed photon's excitation energy hops from molecule to molecule until it reaches a reaction centre, where the hop crosses an electron into a wire of carrier molecules. The heart of the system, Photosystem II, does something remarkable and violent: it uses photon energy, repeatedly, to rip electrons out of water molecules — splitting H₂O into protons, electrons, and oxygen atoms that pair off into O₂, the waste gas every animal breathes. The freed electrons travel an electron transport chain that pumps protons across the membrane, and the protons flowing back through the enzyme ATP synthase produce **ATP**, the cell's rechargeable battery; a second photosystem lifts the electrons to an energy level high enough to load onto the carrier NADPH. The products of Act One — ATP and NADPH — are not sugar; they are the charged batteries that power the next stage, and they last seconds.

## Act Two: Building Sugar from Air

The **Calvin cycle** — named for Melvin Calvin, who traced it with radioactive isotopes in the 1940s, winning a Nobel Prize — runs in the stroma fluid around the thylakoids and needs no light directly, only the batteries Act One made. Its first move is the most consequential protein on Earth: **RuBisCO**, the enzyme that grabs a CO₂ molecule from the air and welds it onto a five-carbon sugar called RuBP, splitting the unstable result into two three-carbon molecules. Successive rounds, fuelled by ATP and NADPH, remodel those fragments into glyceraldehyde, the raw sugar from which the plant builds glucose, starch, cellulose, and everything else it becomes — wood, fruit, root, and the leaf you are looking at is Calvin-cycle output assembled into structure. The cycle must spin its own starting material back into existence: for every three CO₂ fixed, five of six product molecules go to regenerating RuBP, a bookkeeping overhead that makes the whole process slower than it looks in the cartoon. And RuBisCO has a flaw: it cannot perfectly tell CO₂ from O₂, and when it grabs oxygen instead — **photorespiration** — the plant burns energy and loses fixed carbon, a wasteful misfire that, in hot bright conditions where CO₂ runs low inside the leaf, can cost a third of the day's gains.

## The Workarounds Evolution Invented

Because the flaw is environmental, plants have evolved engineering answers worth knowing at the dinner table. **C4 plants** — maize, sugarcane, sorghum — pre-fix CO₂ in outer cells using an enzyme that does not mistake oxygen, then shuttle the concentrated carbon to deep-cell RuBisCO factories operating in effect on CO₂-enriched air: the mechanism that makes maize thrive where rice would overheat, and the basis of ongoing efforts to C4-ify rice for food security. **CAM plants** — cacti, pineapples, the succulent on a windowsill — run the trick in time instead of space: they open their stomata at night, fix carbon into acid stored until daytime, and photosynthesise with the pores shut against the desert sun, which is why a cactus can survive where a cornfield dies. The human angle is not merely botanical: crop yields are photosynthesis yields, and the fraction of sunlight a field actually converts — typically around one to two per cent of the season's incident energy, against a theoretical maximum several times higher — frames the argument about land use, biofuels, and why vertical solar farms beat crops on pure energy-per-hectare. Artificial photosynthesis research tries to copy Act One directly: splitting water with sunlight into clean hydrogen, a field still fighting the cost and lifetime of its catalysts against the cheapness of just buying solar panels.

The perspective worth keeping is that you are downstream of all of it. Every breath of the O₂ that a Photosystem II molecule vented as waste four billion years of evolution ago, every calorie that crossed a food web because some organism could weld air into sugar, and the coal and oil that are buried sunlight fixed in earlier biospheres — the whole energy economy, renewable and fossil alike, is photosynthesis with a time delay. The leaf does it at ambient temperature and pressure, using dirt, water and air, for free.
