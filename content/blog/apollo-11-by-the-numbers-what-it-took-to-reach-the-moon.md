TITLE: Apollo 11 by the Numbers: What It Actually Took to Reach the Moon
EXCERPT: Fifty-seven years on, the Apollo 11 story is usually told as destiny. The numbers tell a better story — of a computer with less memory than a car key, a landing with twenty-five seconds of fuel left, and 400,000 people doing the arithmetic that made one small step possible.
TAGS: Apollo 11, moon, spaceflight, history, engineering, NASA

## The Scale of the Ask

President Kennedy committed to the goal in May 1961, when America's entire spacewalking experience consisted of one astronaut pulling a zipper in a capsule. Apollo 11 landed in July 1969 — eight years, one month. At its peak the Apollo programme employed roughly 400,000 people across 20,000 industrial firms and universities, and consumed about 4 per cent of the federal budget in the mid-1960s, a sum comparable to the entire national research budget of most large countries today. The Saturn V that carried the crew is still the tallest, heaviest, most powerful rocket ever flown operationally: 111 metres, 3,000 tonnes, 34 million newtons of thrust at liftoff — enough to hurl 45 tonnes toward the Moon on a precise four-day ballistic arc.

## The Computer That Fit in a Briefcase

Guidance through all of that ran on the Apollo Guidance Computer: about 32 kilowords of core-rope memory for the flight program, 4 kilowords of RAM, clocked around 1 MHz — orders of magnitude less than the microcontroller in a modern washing machine, and less memory than a car key fob. Core rope memory sounds like a metaphor; it was literal — bits were stored by whether wires passed through or around magnetic cores, woven by hand in what was affectionately called "LOL memory" (Little Old Lady memory) because the weaving was done largely by women factory workers with superb reputations for precision. The physical artifact of the flight software was the program itself: working code *as* hardware, because rewriting a program meant the rope had to be rewoven. When Eagle was falling toward the surface, the computer's job — computing its own position from radar and star sightings sixty times a second — was the same job no autopilot had ever been asked to do in real time on another world.

## Twelve-02 and the Go Decision

The lunar module descended with the crew working the checklist while the computer flew the profile. The most famous abort they did not call: at 102 seconds, the "1202" and "1203" program alarms lit. Guidance officer Jack Garman — who had seen the same codes in a simulator exercise months earlier — called it a computer executive overflow: the rendezvous radar, left in a switch position that fed it a stream of unneeded data, was flooding the CPU faster than it could work, and the computer was deliberately restarting tasks in priority order rather than dying. "Go on those alarms," came the call, in under fifteen seconds of real decision time. The architecture's priority scheme — designed to shed low-priority work and keep flying the rocket — is the reason the alarms were survivable at all. It remains a textbook case of designing software for overload, the same instinct behind the [circuit breakers that keep a search engine responsive when external services fail](https://tukuk.org/blog/caching-strategy-and-circuit-breakers).

Fuel at touchdown: about twenty-five seconds remained between Eagle's footpad and an automated abort that would have thrown the lander back into orbit uncrewed. Armstrong flew the computer's landing target manually past a crater field strewn with tennis-ball-sized rocks, touching down at 1.7 metres per second vertical speed, somewhere between a controlled fall and a controlled miracle.

## The Arithmetic Nobody Saw

The launch window existed because of ballistics, not scheduling meetings. The whole mission was a series of solved two-body problems written in inches of paper: the trans-lunar injection burn to put the stack on a free-return trajectory that would sling them around the Moon and back even if every engine failed afterwards; the rendezvous geometry that let Aldrin dock with Collins overhead while the two modules orbited 110 kilometres above an airless world; the ascent from the Moon, where a failed engine left literally no rescue possible. The 21.5 kilogrammes of Moon rocks brought back, the seven-day round trip, the 500,000-mile odometer reading — all of it built on math that predates the rocket by half a century of celestial mechanics, and hardware that had to be perfect on the first try in a way nothing like it had ever been asked to be.

## What the Numbers Mean Now

Viewed with 2026 distance, Apollo's legacy is less about flags and more about method: mission-critical software with formal priority design, redundancy that expects failure, and an organisation that could integrate two thousand contractor deliverables into one launch date. The Moon is no longer a destination only superpowers can attempt — samples have returned by robot, and commercial landers now touch down where Surveyor once stood, with guidance computers billions of times more capable than the one that survived the 1202 alarms. The Moon still hangs in [the night sky over Earth](https://tukuk.org/blog/a-field-guide-to-your-first-night-of-stargazing), unremarkable and precise, at a distance you can point at with a binocular aim and a steady hand: 384,400 kilometres — a number that meant nothing before 1969, and everything since.
