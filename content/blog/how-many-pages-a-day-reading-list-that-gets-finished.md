## The Sunday-night question with no number attached

Every reading list carries the same silent defect: it says *what* and *by when* but never *how much per day*. So the term runs on feel — and feel is a bad accountant. Some books go unread until the week before the seminar; others get marathoned at a pace that empties them by chapter four. The remedy is not more motivation. Motivation is rarely the missing variable; a number is.

Three inputs decide it: the page count of the thing, the page you are on, and the days left to the deadline. Subtract, divide, round up. Two hundred and thirty-four pages over a fortnight is seventeen a day — a fact that takes eleven seconds to compute and changes what tonight looks like. A free [reading pace calculator](https://tukuk.org/alat/reading-pace) does the division and adds the part feel never supplies: a pace table showing what each speed really costs. Seventeen a day makes the date; ten a day does not, and the table says so in the same flat voice, which is exactly the honesty a reader needs at eleven p.m.

## Why the deadline has to be the starting ingredient

Reading without a date attached drifts to the bottom of every list, because it is permanently important and never urgent. That is why the arithmetic starts from the deadline instead of the material: set the class test, the book-club night, the syllabus checkpoint first, and the pace falls out as a consequence. The deadline converts a wish into a rate, and a rate is the only form of intention a schedule can execute.

Two refinements make the number survivable. **Round up and take the buffer**: a plan that finishes a day early survives the bad week, and seventeen becomes sixteen-plus-a-margin rather than seventeen-plus-panic. **Split the sitting**: recall and comprehension are better at the ends of a session than in the middle of one long drag, so seventeen pages as nine in the morning and eight in the evening genuinely read better than seventeen in one go. For the second half of the problem — what a whole exam season looks like when every subject has a reading pile — the same spreading logic applies across days in [how to plan a study week](https://tukuk.org/blog/how-to-plan-a-study-week-that-survives-real-life).

## Reading for retention, not just for the count

Pages a day only helps if something survives the page. Three habits cost under a minute each and change what the reading is for. **Book the close:** at the end of every sitting, write two sentences on what the chunk argued, from memory — the smallest possible act of [active recall](https://tukuk.org/blog/active-recall-why-testing-yourself-beats-re-reading), and the difference between having-read and having-got. **Mark the load-bearers, sparsely:** if a chapter yields more than a handful of sentences worth keeping, the highlighting is decoration; a [text summarizer](https://tukuk.org/alat/summarizer) can show you what frequency-based selection looks like as a sanity check on your own eye. **Know your rate:** an unfamiliar 300-page book with a test at the end of a fortnight is a different arithmetic than a fluent re-read, and honest wpm estimates turn a reading pile into dated commitments — a [reading time estimator](https://tukuk.org/alat/reading-time) puts a minute figure on any passage before you commit the evening to it.

## The number that ends arguments

The quiet victory of doing the division is that the question changes shape. Not *will I get through it* — a question with no answer, guaranteed to produce guilt and procrastination — but *seventeen today, then seventeen tomorrow*, a question the evening can actually win. Lists get finished by people who know their rate. The cover has the page count, the calendar has the date; the only missing number was always the one in between, and it takes eleven seconds to find.

```

```
