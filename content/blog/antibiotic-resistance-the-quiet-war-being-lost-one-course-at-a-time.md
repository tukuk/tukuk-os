## Darwin, Running a Hospital-Scale Experiment

An antibiotic does not "create" resistance; it selects for it. Any large bacterial population contains a few mutants or acquired-gene carriers that survive a given drug — by chemically dismantling it, pumping it out with efflux pumps, altering the target site the drug binds, or growing behind a thickened wall. Treat an infection and the susceptible billions die while the resistant handful inherit the emptied tissue. The medicine that cured the patient has just run a selection experiment on the pathogen, and the patient's own body becomes the culture vessel from which the next, harder infection spreads. Every course of antibiotics, used wisely or foolishly, drives this cycle; the only variable is the speed. Resistance genes are mobile in the literal sense — plasmids and transposons shuttle them between species in the gut of a single patient, so a harmless bacterium trained by one drug can hand the knowledge to a deadly one. Bacteria have been doing horizontal gene transfer for three billion years; our medical timeline is a rounding error in their playbook. [How life classifies and adapts](https://tukuk.org/blog/how-scientists-classify-life-from-linnaeus-to-dna-trees) at this scale explains why the microbial world is so much faster to evolve than anything the antibiotic era assumed.

The human toll now matches the mechanism. Conservative global estimates put deaths directly attributable to resistant infections at roughly 1.2 million a year, with nearly five million deaths associated with them — projections place it among the top causes of death by 2050. Gonorrhoea, drug-resistant tuberculosis, bacterial pneumonias, and post-surgical sepsis are the front lines. The economic fear is quieter: an appendix operation, a C-section, a joint replacement, and a course of chemotherapy all assume that if bacteria invade, a drug exists that works. Lose that assumption and modern medicine's risk ledger changes fundamentally — the era's greatest gains (safe childbirth, safe surgery, long transplants) were leveraged on a small shelf of molecules, and the shelf is being drawn down.

## Why the Pipeline Ran Dry

If resistance is selection pressure, the answer should be: invent new antibiotics. The science partly can; the economics will not. New antibiotics are cheap to make when they work, so the rational medical use is to prescribe them *sparingly*, reserved for resistant cases — which caps their sales volume, guarantees obsolescence by resistance, and makes a billion-dollar development programme a terrible investment. The result is a market failure at the exact point where public health needs supply: since the 1980s the flow of novel classes has thinned almost to nothing, while resistance to nearly every existing class has climbed. Big pharma largely exited the field in the 2010s after writing down billion-dollar acquisitions. This is why policy experiments now include "delinked" prizes and subscription models — paying for the *availability* of a new antibiotic rather than the volume sold, treating the drug like a fire service you pay to keep ready rather than a product you profit from using.

## The Four Battlefields

The war is not lost, and the fronts are specific. **Stewardship**: reducing unnecessary prescriptions (antibiotics do nothing to viral colds, yet patient expectation drove prescribing for decades), banning growth-promotion doses in livestock — historically the largest single tonnage of antibiotics on Earth — and shortening treatment courses where evidence supports it; a decade of European farm-policy cuts has shown measurable resistance declines in pigs and poultry, proof the lever works. **Surveillance**: genomic tracking of resistance genes in hospitals, farms and rivers, because the genes move through all three, and a sewage plume is an early-warning station. **Alternatives**: phage therapy — viruses that hunt specific bacteria — moving from Georgian clinic tradition into regulated trials; monoclonal antibodies, vaccines against pneumonia and MRSA in trials, and faecal microbiota transplant as a way to re-collonise a gut so resistant organisms cannot take hold. **Discovery**: the slow one — mining soil bacteria, deep-sea sediment, and the "unculturable" majority of microbial life with new cultivation techniques and machine-assisted screening, which has already produced halicin, an antibiotic scaffold found by an AI model that worked on resistant lab strains.

The individual's weapon in all of this is boring and real: take the course you are prescribed for the infection you actually have, never share or keep leftovers, never demand antibiotics for a cold, vaccinate when offered (every infection prevented is an infection that cannot evolve or spread resistance), and wash hands — the cheapest epidemiological intervention ever invented. Antibiotic resistance is the rare catastrophe that is fully mechanistic, fully predictable, and fully human-driven; Fleming himself raised the warning from the podium in 1945. The bill arriving eighty years later is not a mystery. It is the receipt for using the most useful drugs in medicine as if selection pressure were someone else's problem.
