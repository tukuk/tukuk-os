## The Grid: Height Times Shape

Every cloud you will ever see fits a two-axis grid. The first axis is **height**: high clouds (above roughly six kilometres, made of ice crystals), middle clouds (two to six kilometres, mixed water and ice), and low clouds (under two kilometres, pure water droplets). The second axis is **shape**: *stratiform* — layered, sheet-like, formed when a wide slab of air rises gently — or *cumuliform* — heaped, bubbly, formed when small pockets of air rise fast. Multiply three heights by two shapes and you get the Latin scaffolding Luke Howard gave the sky in 1803: cirro-, alto-, and strato- combined with -cumulus and -stratus. The naming is not decoration; each label encodes the physical process that built the cloud, which is exactly why each label carries forecast information. [The original classification of cloud types](https://tukuk.org/blog/cloud-types-and-what-they-mean-for-weather) covers the basics — this guide goes species by species, promises by promise.

## The High Ones: Ice Crystal Messengers

**Cirrus** — the mare's-tail wisps — are wind shear made visible: ice crystals falling from ascending air get stretched into hooks by the jet stream hundreds of kilometres above. Thin, patchy cirrus means nothing much. But cirrus thickening and lowering over a day or two is the classic leading edge of a warm front hundreds of kilometres away — the first hint that a rain system is marching toward you.

**Cirrostratus** — the milky veil — produces the halo around sun or moon, caused by light refracting through flat hexagonal ice plates. See a halo, and watch the veil thicken: rain is a common outcome within twenty-four hours. **Cirrocumulus** — the "mackerel sky" of tiny ripples — signals instability at altitude; the old rhyme "mackerel sky, not wet nor dry" is half right: it often precedes the front rather than the rain itself.

## The Middle Ones: Layers of Doubt

**Altocumulus** — sheep-back flocks of grey-white puffs — often forms on humid summer mornings and says the air is conditionally unstable: fair now, with a chance of afternoon thunderstorms if the sun heats the ground enough. **Altostratus** — a featureless grey sheet that dims the sun like a ground-glass bulb — means a deep, steady lift of moist air; when you cannot see the sun's disc through it but the sky is not yet dark, rain is usually hours away. If it thickens into **nimbostratus** — dark, shapeless, soaking the view — the steady rain is simply overhead now, and will be for a while.

## The Low Ones: Neighbours You Can Trust

**Stratus** — a uniform grey blanket with a flat base, often chewing the tops of hills — is a fog that never lifted, or a fog that will form again at dusk if it clears. It brings drizzle at worst, and it usually tears apart mid-morning as heat mixes in from below. **Stratocumulus** — the lumpy grey-and-blue quilt that dominates the world's oceans on satellite photos — is the most common cloud on Earth, a stable lid of shallow convection; from the ground it promises nothing dramatic. **Cumulus humilis** — the flat-based, cauliflower-topped fair-weather heaps of afternoon — are literally your local thermals made visible, and their bases mark the lifting condensation level, the height at which rising surface air finally gets saturated. Watch their growth rate: cumulus that keep growing vertically into **cumulonimbus** with an anvil top are the same physics escalated into a storm, a process detailed in [how lightning forms](https://tukuk.org/blog/how-lightning-forms-and-why-it-strikes-where-it-does).

## Reading Motion, Not Just Form

The last skill is temporal: clouds are verbs, not nouns. A single photograph tells you what exists; ten minutes of watching tells you what is arriving. Note whether the veil is thickening or thinning, whether the cumuliform towers are spreading downwind into anvils, and whether the lowest cloud layer is descending toward the ground — morning stratus lowering to fog level is the classic clear-evening sign, because falling clouds mean the air is drying from below as radiative cooling draws the base down. Learn the ten forms and this one habit of watching change, and you hold a genuinely useful personal forecast barometer that needs no app, works while your phone is dead, and — the reason meteorology started it two centuries before satellites — has never once required you to trust anyone else's sky.
