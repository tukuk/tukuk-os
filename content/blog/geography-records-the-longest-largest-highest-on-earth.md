## The Rivers: Longest Is a Genuine Dispute

**The Nile** is conventionally crowned the longest river at roughly **6,650 km**, flowing north from Lake Victoria's region to the Mediterranean. But **the Amazon** — about 6,400 to 6,992 km depending on which source you accept and which glacier counts as "the start" — has some measured longer, and undisputably carries the most water by a mile: on the order of **200,000 cubic metres per second** discharged at the mouth — a fifth of all river water reaching the oceans, more than several of the next-largest rivers combined — and broad enough that its plume keeps the ocean fresh kilometres offshore.

The lesson geography teachers gloss: "longest river" is a measurement of a *curve in three dimensions with a debatable starting point*. When sources disagree, they are usually both honest — river-length science is genuinely hard.

## The Lakes: Biggest Is Political Geography

By surface, the **Caspian Sea** is the largest enclosed body of water on Earth at roughly **371,000 km²** — bigger than Germany — and it is called a sea while being a landlocked lake by every definition except naming tradition (its water is saline, fed by rivers, with no natural outlet to an ocean).

The truly giant freshwater lake by volume is **Lake Baikal** in Siberia: about **1,642 m deep**, holding roughly 23,600 km³ of liquid fresh water — around **one-fifth of all unfrozen fresh surface water on the planet** in a single rift valley, and the world's oldest lake at 25+ million years. Upper + Victoria + Huron combined do not match it; Baikal is deep where the others are wide.

The highest large lake you can stand next to with easy access: **Titicaca** on the Peru–Bolivia border, about 3,812 m, and the navigable lake record-holder at that altitude.

## The Deserts: Antarctica Wins and Nobody Expects It

A desert is defined by precipitation, not heat. By that definition the largest desert on Earth is **Antarctica**, about **14.2 million km²**, technically the driest continent — its Dry Valleys have gone millions of years without rain. The largest *hot* desert is the **Sahara** at around 9.2 million km², an area the size of China. The rainiest measured place on Earth is **Mawsynram, India**, on the monsoon-facing edge of the Khasi Hills, averaging around 11,700 mm of rain a year — close to twelve metres of water falling annually, versus the Atacama's decades-long stretches of essentially none.

## The Heights and the Lows

**Everest** — 8,849 m, the 2020 joint Nepal–China survey value that replaced the older 8,848 — is the highest point above sea level. That qualifier matters, because the "tallest mountain" from base to summit is **Denali** in Alaska (about 5,500 m of relief from its base plain), and the farthest point from Earth's centre is **Chimborazo** in Ecuador, an equatorial bulge beneficiary: sea level is not a sphere.

The lowest point on land is the shore of the **Dead Sea**, roughly **430 m below sea level** and still dropping as inflow shrinks — the exposed salt flats are the planet's most visible water-billboard.

The longest mountain range on land is the **Andes**, about 7,000 km down the spine of South America, containing the highest peak outside Asia (**Aconcagua**, 6,961 m). The *longest mountain range of all* is the mid-ocean ridge system — around 65,000 km of volcanic seams running through every ocean, 90% of it never seen by daylight.

Seven-summit trivia with a twist: the highest peak on an island is **Puncak Jaya** in New Guinea (4,884 m), and the tallest free-standing mountain on Earth — rising from plain, no range behind it — is **Kilimanjaro** at 5,895 m.

## The Islands: Why Australia Beats Greenland

The largest island is **Greenland** (~2.16 million km²) — *if* you accept the convention that Australia is a continent, not an island. The real reason is tectonic: Australia rides its own continental plate; Greenland does not, so geographers grant it the continent-minus title. New Guinea is the second-largest island, and Borneo — shared by three countries — the third, and the only place on Earth where wild orangutans still live on both sides of a border.

## The Extremes Nobody Visits

**Point Nemo**, the oceanic pole of inaccessibility in the South Pacific, is the place farthest from any land — about 2,700 km to the nearest island shore. When the ISS passes overhead, the humans closest to Point Nemo are, quite often, *the astronauts themselves*; the surrounding ocean is used as a cemetery for spent spacecraft because nothing is there to be hit.

And the lowest land point you can walk to without a submarine? The Dead Sea shore again — a swimming experience in water so dense with salt you cannot sink, in a landscape where every mineral crust spells out evaporation.
