TAGS: geography, roads, travel, islands, connectivity

## The Road Network Has Tears in It

We talk about "the road network" as if it were a fabric, but it is a graph — and graphs have disconnected pieces. The most famous seams are natural:

- **Borneo.** Malaysia's two halves look adjacent on a map — Kuching and Kuala Lumpur are barely 1,000 km apart by air — but the South China Sea sits between them and no bridge exists. Drive from Sarawak to KL and every router tells you the truth: impossible. The road journey means putting the vehicle on a ship — the usual corridor runs via Sabah and Labuan, or down through Singapore to the peninsula — or just flying.
- **Ireland, Britain, Japan, New Zealand, Iceland, Sri Lanka, Madagascar** — entire nations where the car must swim. Britain's seam is now patched by the Channel Tunnel, which carries trains, not cars directly — your car still rides the shuttle.
- **The Korean Peninsula** — one land mass, effectively two road islands: the inter-Korean border is closed to through traffic, so a router must refuse "drive from Busan to Pyongyang" as firmly as if it were an ocean.

Where water is narrow, engineers build the patch: Denmark–Sweden by bridge, France–England by tunnel, Thailand's south connected to the Malay peninsula at the **Kra Isthmus** — 44 km of land separating two seas, which is why you can drive from Singapore all the way to Turkey but not one metre further east into Borneo. Every isthmus is a place where a canal proposal (always controversial) has lived for a century, because a canal moves the seam permanently.

## Man-Made Seams: Borders That Close

The graph breaks on paper too. The Russia–Georgia roads were shut for long stretches; Spain–Gibraltar has an actual border fence; Venezuela's land connections swing depending on diplomacy. A route planner's honest answer to a closed border is "no route", and the ones that quietly pretend otherwise generate the classic Google Maps driving-a-driver-into-a-lake behaviour when the statistical model treats a ferry link as an edge that ships use. (The inverse also happens: Maps once recommended crossing the Adriatic on a cycling route because the two coasts were both "connected" in an old dataset.)

## The Fun Geography It Teaches

Every island is a case study: **islands have ferry ports, peninsulas have necks, isthmuses have canal dreams, and landlocked countries have transit agreements.** When you ask "how far is it", three different correct numbers exist — the great-circle distance, the driving distance, and the realistic door-to-door distance including sea crossings. Disagreement between them is not error; it is the coastline showing.
