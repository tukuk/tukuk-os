TITLE: Why We Have Seasons — It Is Not Because Earth Gets Closer to the Sun
EXCERPT: The most common astronomy misconception on Earth: seasons are caused by the 23.4-degree tilt of our axis, not distance. The proof is that January, Earth's closest approach, is winter in the north.
TAGS: seasons, earth, astronomy, science, education

## The Myth That Will Not Die

Ask people why it is warmer in summer and you will often hear "the Earth is closer to the Sun." The actual numbers destroy that idea: Earth reaches **perihelion** — its closest point to the Sun, about 147 million km — in early January, right in the middle of the northern winter. At aphelion in early July it is about 152 million km away, roughly 3.3 percent farther, while northern summer blazes. Distance cannot be the driver when the closest approach lands in the coldest northern month.

## The Real Cause: Tilt, Not Distance

Earth's rotation axis leans 23.4 degrees away from perpendicular to its orbital plane, and — crucially — that axis keeps pointing in nearly the same direction in space as we orbit (toward Polaris). So half the year the northern hemisphere leans sunward, and half the year it leans away.

Two things happen to the sunlight when a hemisphere leans toward the Sun:

1. **More direct rays.** Sunlight hits the ground at a steeper angle, concentrating the same beam of energy onto a smaller surface. Think of a torch held straight down versus tilted: the tilted patch of light is bigger and dimmer.
2. **Longer days.** The sunward hemisphere spends more hours above the horizon each rotation, adding many more hours of heating.

Lean away from the Sun and both effects reverse: shallow-angle light and short days. That combination — intensity times duration — is the season.

## Check It Against the Calendar

- **June solstice (~20–21 June):** north pole tilted fully sunward. Midnight Sun inside the Arctic Circle; shortest day south of the equator.
- **December solstice (~21–22):** the reverse. Southern summer peaks while the north bundles up.
- **March and September equinoxes (~20 and ~22):** neither pole leans sunward; day and night come close to equal everywhere on Earth.

Notice the symmetry: when it is Christmas in the northern hemisphere, Australians are having barbecue summers. If distance drove seasons, the whole planet would warm and cool together — and it does not.

## Why the Tilt Itself Matters So Much

Earth's climate zones, the jet streams, the monsoons, the migration of whales and the flowering of wheat all track the seasonal march of the sunward lean. The tilt has wandered between roughly 22.1 and 24.5 degrees over tens of thousands of years (a cycle of about 41,000 years) and those slow swings, together with changes in orbit shape and wobble direction, reorganise the pacing of ice ages — the Milankovitch cycles.

A world with zero tilt would have seasons only by latitude: permanent tropic heat, permanent polar cold, no calendar of migration or harvest. A 90-degree tilt would bake each pole in turn. Our 23.4 degrees is the setting that gives mid-latitudes their four distinct chapters.

## Watch the Geometry Yourself

You can see the tilt's signature without instruments: track the noon shadow of a stick for a year — its length swings with the Sun's height, and the extremes land exactly at the solstices. Our sky page at [tukuk.org/nasa/sky](https://tukuk.org/nasa/sky) explains what to look for tonight, and the science primer at [tukuk.org/study/science](https://tukuk.org/study/science) collects the core ideas behind orbits, light and heat in one page.

Seasons are a story of angle and daylight hours — written by a leaning planet that never stops pointing its axis at the same distant star.
