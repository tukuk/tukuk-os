## The Container That Redrew the Map

Start with the most profitable committee decision in history. Malcom McLean proved the container idea in 1956 with a converted tanker and fifty-eight boxes, but the revolution went **global** only in the late 1960s, when the ISO agreed the box dimensions — the 20- and 40-foot units, corner fittings and stacking strengths still governing every port on Earth. Before that, stevedores broke cargoes by hand, ships spent more time in dock than at sea, and trade cost multiples of what it does now. The container's revolution was not steel; it was **geometry agreed in advance**: every crane, truck, train and warehouse on four continents could be designed assuming the same corner castings. Same logic, same invisibility: railway gauge almost decided which nations' armies moved faster in 1914; pallet sizes decide whether a fridge arrives undamaged; and the Metre Convention of 1875 — one of the first great international technical treaties — is the reason a German screw fits a Japanese engine.

## How the Internet Is a Stack of Standards

The web you are reading this on is standards all the way down. TCP/IP, described by Cerf and Kahn in 1974, is the agreed rulebook for packet delivery; HTTP is the agreed way a browser asks and a server answers — the request/response cycle unpacked in our [HTTPS and TLS explainer](https://tukuk.org/blog/how-https-keeps-the-web-private-tls-in-plain-terms); DNS is the agreed phone book, secured by [DNS over HTTPS](https://tukuk.org/blog/dns-over-https-explained); HTML itself is a W3C standard. The revealing part: the internet won *because* it was an open standard anyone could implement, while its rivals — proprietary networks from IBM, AT&T, France's Minitel — were walls around gardens. The engineers who chose "free for everyone, forever" over a monopoly profit in the protocol gave the open network its inevitable victory, and set the template for every success since: USB (one connector, all brands), Bluetooth, PDF, JPEG.

## Who Actually Writes These Things

Standards bodies are the least famous powerful institutions on Earth: ISO in Geneva (founded 1947, 160+ member bodies), the IEC for electrical things, the IEEE for electronics, the IETF for the internet's protocols — and thousands of sector groups from banking messaging to 3G spectrum. Their process is deliberately dull: public drafts, documented dissent, consensus before publication, because nobody is forced to adopt any of it. A standard is just a very well-argued suggestion; its power is that being first, documented and widely reviewed makes ignoring it expensive. Patents enter the bargain too: bodies like IEEE require essential patents to be licensed fairly, which is one reason mobile phones work on any network instead of each vendor holding the world hostage.

## Why You Should Care About an Unsexy Topic

Standards are the difference between a tool and a system — and they shape what you can build alone. The free data APIs collected in [no-key world data for developers](https://tukuk.org/blog/free-world-data-apis-no-api-key-needed) all speak standard formats because standard formats are how small projects survive; the everyday tools at [tukuk.org/alat](https://tukuk.org/alat) and the tech cluster at [tukuk.org/technology](https://tukuk.org/technology) run on the same principle. Watch for the warning signs, too: fragmentation (a world where every charger differs is a world that stopped agreeing) and capture (when one company's private format becomes everyone's dependency). Civilisation is mostly people agreeing about interfaces — and the next time two things you own click together perfectly, that click is a small, boring, magnificent treaty.
